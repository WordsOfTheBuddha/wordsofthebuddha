Starting with the canonical importance of the practice of mindfulness, this article examines mindfulness through the historical context in which the Buddha describes it. It then tests the alignment of modern attempts to explain mindfulness with the canonical presentation. It highlights the harms that adopting a fixed definition of mindfulness can lead to, and proposes a feedback loop that a practitioner can adopt to gradually arrive at a less wrong understanding of mindfulness.

#### Mindfulness’s Recourse is Liberation

If mindfulness (sati) in the context of the Buddha’s teachings were to be understood as it truly is—how it comes to be, what leads to its decline, how it is to be cultivated, the diversity within its cultivation, and how its outcomes are to be measured—one would be standing at the doorstep of liberation. In an exchange with the brahmin Uṇṇābha, the Buddha describes its relation to liberation in this manner:

> _“Sir Gotama, these five faculties each have a distinct field and a distinct domain, and do not experience each other's fields and domains. What five? The eye faculty, the ear faculty, the nose faculty, the tongue faculty, and the body faculty. Now, sir Gotama, as these five faculties have distinct fields, distinct domains, and do not experience each other's fields and domains, what is it that they take recourse in? And what is it that experiences their fields and domains?”_
>
> _“Brahmin, for these five faculties that have distinct fields, distinct domains, and do not experience each other's fields and domains, the mind is their recourse, and the mind experiences their fields and domains.”_
>
> _“And sir Gotama, what is it that the mind takes recourse in?”_
>
> _“The mind, brahmin, takes recourse in mindfulness (sati).”_
>
> _“And sir Gotama, what is it that mindfulness (sati) takes recourse in?”_
>
> _“Mindfulness (sati), brahmin, takes recourse in liberation.”_
>
> _“And sir Gotama, what is it that liberation takes recourse in?”_
>
> _“Liberation, brahmin, takes recourse in Nibbāna.”_

— [SN 48.42 Uṇṇābha sutta ¶3 - ¶10](/sn48.42#3-10)

Elsewhere, the Buddha states the importance of the practice of mindfulness:

> _“This is the one-way for the purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow and lamentation, for the passing away of discomfort and distress, for the achievement of the method, for the personal realization of Nibbāna, that is, the **four establishments of mindfulness** (cattāro satipaṭṭhānā).”_

— [SN 47.1 Ambapāli sutta ¶3](/sn47.1#3)

> _“Indeed, if anyone should develop these four establishments of mindfulness in such a way for seven years, one of two fruits can be expected: either final knowledge [of the complete wearing away of the taints] here and now, or, if there is any residual clinging for existence, the state of non-returning._

> _Let alone seven years. If anyone should develop **these four establishments of mindfulness** (ime cattāro satipaṭṭhāne) in such a way for six years... five years... four years... three years... two years... one year... Let alone one year. If anyone should develop these four establishments of mindfulness in such a way for seven months, one of two fruits can be expected: either final knowledge of full awakening here and now or, if there is any residual clinging for existence, the state of non-returning. Let alone seven months. If anyone should develop these four establishments of mindfulness in such a way for six months... five months... four months... three months... two months... one month... half a month... Let alone half a month. If anyone should develop these four establishments of mindfulness in such a way for one week, one of two fruits can be expected: either final knowledge of full awakening here and now or, if there is any residual clinging for existence, the state of non-returning.”_

— [MN 10 Satipaṭṭhāna sutta ¶110-111](/mn10#110-111)

Given the potency that the Buddha describes this practice of mindfulness as having, where even a mere seven days of cultivation can lead to the attainment of final knowledge of full awakening here and now or to the state of non-returning, we should be interested in understanding it such that it is clear how it comes to be, what leads to its decline, how it is to be cultivated, the diversity within its cultivation, and how its outcomes are to be measured. Even if that understanding took seven years to arrive at, it would be worth the effort.

And we can expect the Buddha, who describes its potency in this manner, to have made clear what it is. What we find in the suttas is that he describes it through the one who has it, and through how it is dwelt in, rather than as something graspable by itself.

In his 2011 article "What does mindfulness really mean?" Bhikkhu Bodhi in reference to the suttas states: _“Yet, when we consult the texts to find out what they mean by sati, what we mostly encounter, instead of lucid explanations, are operational demonstrations that indicate, in practical terms, how sati functions. It is from these that we must tease out the word’s implications, testing them against each other and evaluating them by personal reflection and experience.”_

#### Defined by the One who Has It

This is how the Buddha describes the faculty of mindfulness:

> _“And what is the **faculty of mindfulness** (satindriyaṁ)? Here, a disciple of the Noble Ones is **mindful** (satimā), possessing supreme **mindfulness and skillfulness** (satinepakkena), **remembering** (saritā) and **recollecting** (anussaritā) what was done and said long ago. This is called the faculty of mindfulness.”_

— [SN 48.9 Paṭhama vibhaṅga sutta ¶4](/sn48.9#4)

To define the faculty of mindfulness here, the Buddha uses four terms that tie back to mindfulness, namely: mindful (satimā), **mindfulness** _and skillfulness_ (**sati**_nepakkena_), remembering (saritā) and recollecting (anussaritā). The last two terms, saritā and anussaritā, are agent nouns derived from the verb sarati, ‘to remember’ or ‘to recollect’; the first is simple, the second is prefixed with anu, which suggests repetition or closeness. So while the English rendering for these do not use mindfulness, the Pāli terms show the relation to it.

In the next discourse, the Buddha is seen describing the faculty of mindfulness as:

> _“And what is the faculty of mindfulness? Here, a disciple of the Noble Ones is mindful, possessing supreme mindfulness and skillfulness, remembering and recollecting what was done and said long ago. He dwells observing the **body** (kāya) in and of itself, with continuous effort, clearly aware and **mindful** (satimā), having removed craving and distress with regard to the world. He dwells observing **felt experience** (vedanā) in and of itself ... **mind** (citta) in and of itself ... **mental qualities** (dhammā) in and of themselves, with continuous effort, clearly aware and **mindful** (satimā), having removed craving and distress with regard to the world. This is called the faculty of mindfulness.”_

— [SN 48.10 Dutiya vibhaṅga sutta ¶4](/sn48.10#4)

Here, the Buddha uses the previous definition and then further adds the formula of the four establishments of mindfulness. The quality of being mindful is presented alongside other mental qualities, of continuous effort (ātāpī), being clearly aware (sampajāna), and with the stance of continuously observing (anupassī) in each of the four establishments of mindfulness.

More concise versions are then presented in:

> _“And where is the faculty of mindfulness to be seen? In the four establishments of mindfulness—here the faculty of mindfulness is to be seen.”_

— [SN 48.8 Daṭṭhabba sutta ¶4](/sn48.8#4)

> _“And what is the faculty of mindfulness? Whatever mindfulness one acquires by undertaking the four establishments of mindfulness—this is called the faculty of mindfulness.”_

— [SN 48.11 Paṭilābha sutta ¶4](/sn48.11#4)

In the article (2011: 24), B. Bodhi interprets the SN 48.10’s definition of the faculty of mindfulness to also include the four establishments of mindfulness to be an indication that “the compilers of the texts were not satisfied with the simple definition in terms of memory but felt the need to supplement it with another definition that underscores its connection with meditation practice.”



There is another discourse where the Buddha describes how the faculty of mindfulness comes to be in a disciple of the Noble Ones (ariyasāvaka):

> _“Sāriputta, a disciple of the Noble Ones who is entirely devoted to the Tathāgata and has complete confidence in him would not have any doubt or uncertainty about the Tathāgata or the Tathāgata’s teaching._

> _For, Sāriputta, it is to be expected that a disciple of the Noble Ones who has faith will dwell with energy aroused for the abandoning of unwholesome qualities and the acquisition of wholesome qualities; he is firm, steadfast in striving, and never lays down the yoke of wholesome qualities. That energy of his, Sāriputta, is his faculty of energy._

> _For, Sāriputta, it is to be expected that a disciple of the Noble Ones who has faith and has aroused energy will be mindful, possessing supreme mindfulness and skillfulness, remembering and recollecting what was done and said long ago. That mindfulness of his, Sāriputta, is his faculty of mindfulness.”_

— [SN 48.50 Āpaṇa sutta ¶10 - ¶12](/sn48.50#10-12)

In yet another discourse, we get a hint from the Buddha that such clear delineation

On one hand, it is quite easy to experientially understand the five sense faculties of eye, ear, nose, tongue, and body. When they're spoken of, when their field and domain is spoken of, it is quite precise to understand what each of these areas is.

On the other hand, it is not at all possible to grasp the meaning of liberation without having experienced it.

Mind and mindfulness sit somewhere in between. Of the two, it is easy to know mind through the various states it experiences: being with passion, being free of it, being with hate, being free of it, being with delusion, being free of it, of dullness, of scattering, of distractedness, of collectedness. To the extent that each of these mental states is experienced, to that extent one can say that one understands the mind.

A correct understanding of mindfulness then is similarly only to the extent that it has been practiced and known. To the extent that mindfulness has actually been present and dwelt in, to that extent one can say that one understands it. And yet it is this that the Buddha said the mind takes recourse in, and which itself takes recourse in liberation. A correct understanding of it is vital. Without that, one can take a repeated watching of the breath, or a noting of things, to be the whole of it.

There is a line of inquiry here that can be fruitful. What follows is only a starting point, meant to stir up the idea that mindfulness may not be fully understood, and that its correct understanding is vital to the practice. Some questions along this line:

- How is it defined?
- What are the prior practices to taking up the practice of mindfulness?
- How should the outcome of mindfulness be measured? It should be possible to do this, as this is part of the eightfold path, and so it should be possible to cultivate it. A progress and growth should be visible in it.
- What is the relation between faith and mindfulness, energy and mindfulness, right view and mindfulness, radical attention (yoniso manasikāra) and mindfulness?
- Can one have mindfulness while being with reflection (vitakka) and examination (vicāra)? Can there be mindfulness with it?
- Is mindfulness a monotonous repeated exercise of watching the breath, or of noting things? Can one change subjects while being in mindfulness?
- Is it possible to arrive at right mindfulness without having right livelihood?

This line of inquiry can be fruitful. The point is to stir the sense that mindfulness may not be fully understood, and that a correct understanding is vital.
