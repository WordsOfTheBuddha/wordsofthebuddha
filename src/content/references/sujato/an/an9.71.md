title: Hard-heartedness
description: The Buddha describes five kinds of hard-heartedness—doubt about the teacher, the teaching, the Sangha, or the training, and being angry and closed off toward one's spiritual companions—each preventing the mind from inclining to effort, and says to give them up by developing the four kinds of mindfulness meditation.

<!-- @segment an9.71:1.1 -->

“Mendicants, there are five kinds of hard-heartedness.

<!-- @segment an9.71:1.2 -->

What five?

<!-- @segment an9.71:1.3 -->

Firstly, a mendicant has doubts about the Teacher. They’re uncertain, undecided, and lacking confidence.

<!-- @segment an9.71:1.4 -->

This being so, their mind doesn’t incline toward keenness, commitment, persistence, and striving.

<!-- @segment an9.71:1.5 -->

This is the first kind of hard-heartedness.

<!-- @segment an9.71:2.1 -->

Furthermore, a mendicant has doubts about the teaching …

<!-- @segment an9.71:2.2 -->

the Saṅgha …

<!-- @segment an9.71:2.3 -->

the training …

<!-- @segment an9.71:2.4 -->

A mendicant is angry and upset with their spiritual companions, resentful and closed off.

<!-- @segment an9.71:2.5 -->

This being so, their mind doesn’t incline toward keenness, commitment, persistence, and striving.

<!-- @segment an9.71:2.6 -->

This is the fifth kind of hard-heartedness.

<!-- @segment an9.71:3.1 -->

To give up these five kinds of hard-heartedness you should develop the four kinds of mindfulness meditation. …”
