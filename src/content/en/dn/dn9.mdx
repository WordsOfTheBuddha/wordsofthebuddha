### 1. With the Wanderer Poṭṭhapāda

Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling at Sāvatthi, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

Now at that time, the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda was staying in |Mallikā::wife of King Pasenadi; lit. jasmine [mallikā]|’s park, at the single-halled debating hall surrounded by a row of ebony trees, with a large assembly of wanderers, about three hundred wanderers. Then the Blessed One, having dressed early in the morning, took his bowl and robe and entered Sāvatthi for alms.

Then it occurred to the Blessed One: “It is too early to walk for alms in Sāvatthi. Why don’t I approach the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda in Mallikā’s park, at the single-halled debating hall surrounded by a row of ebony trees?” Then the Blessed One approached Mallikā’s park, at the single-halled debating hall surrounded by a row of ebony trees.

Now at that time, the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda was sitting with a large assembly of wanderers who were making an uproar, a loud and great noise, engaging in various kinds of |pointless talk::unfruitful talk, aimless talk, worldly talk [tiracchānakathā]|, namely: tales of kings, thieves, and ministers; tales of armies, dangers, and battles; talk of food, drink, clothing, beds, garlands, and perfumes; talk of relatives, vehicles, villages, towns, cities, and countries; tales of women and heroes; street talk and well talk; tales of the departed; various kinds of chatter; speculation about the world and the ocean; and talk of being and non-being.

The wanderer Poṭṭhapāda saw the Blessed One coming from afar. Seeing him, he hushed his own assembly: “Good sirs, be quiet; good sirs, do not make a sound. The ascetic Gotama is coming. That venerable one loves quiet and praises quiet. Perhaps, if he sees a quiet assembly, he might think to approach.” Then those wanderers fell silent.

Then the Blessed One approached the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda. Then the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda said to the Blessed One: “Let the Blessed One come, venerable sir. Welcome to the Blessed One, venerable sir. It has been a long time since the Blessed One has made the opportunity to come here. Let the Blessed One sit down; this seat is prepared.”

The Blessed One sat down on the prepared seat. The wanderer Poṭṭhapāda, having taken a lower seat, sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the Blessed One said to the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda: “Poṭṭhapāda, what conversation were you just now sitting together engaged in? And what was your discussion that was interrupted?”

#### 1.1. The Ultimate Cessation of Perception

When this was said, the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda said to the Blessed One: “Let that be, venerable sir, the talk that we were sitting discussing just now. It will not be difficult for the Blessed One to hear about this talk later.

In recent days, venerable sir, and days prior to that, when ascetics and brahmins of various sects were seated and gathered together in the debating hall, a discussion arose concerning the |ultimate cessation of perception::total extinction of consciousness [abhisaññānirodha]|: ‘How, sirs, does the total cessation of perception come to be?’

Therein, some said: ‘Without cause and without condition, a person’s |perceptions::the mental process of recognizing, conceptualizing, and interpreting sensory experience; third of the five aggregates [saññā]| arise and cease. At whatever time they arise, at that time one is percipient. At whatever time they cease, at that time one is non-percipient.’ In this way, some describe the ultimate cessation of perception.

Another said to him: ‘That cannot be so, sir. For perception is a person’s |self::soul, permanent entity, enduring personal essence [attā]|. It comes and goes. At whatever time it comes, at that time one is percipient. At whatever time it goes, at that time one is non-percipient.’ In this way, some describe the ultimate cessation of perception.

Another said to him: ‘That cannot be so, sir. For there are ascetics and brahmins of great psychic power and great might. They draw perception into this person and draw it out. At whatever time they draw it in, at that time one is percipient. At whatever time they draw it out, at that time one is non-percipient.’ In this way, some describe the ultimate cessation of perception.

Another said to him: ‘That cannot be so, sir. For there are deities of great psychic power and great might. They draw perception into this person and draw it out. At whatever time they draw it in, at that time one is percipient. At whatever time they draw it out, at that time one is non-percipient.’ In this way, some describe the ultimate cessation of perception.

Then, venerable sir, a |recollection::lucid retention and bringing to mind; the faculty of memory [sati]| of the Blessed One arose in me: ‘Ah, indeed the Blessed One, ah, indeed the Accomplished One, who is so skilled in these mental states!’ The Blessed One, venerable sir, is skilled, the Blessed One is an expert regarding the ultimate cessation of perception. How then, venerable sir, does the ultimate cessation of perception come to be?”

#### 1.2. Causality in the Arising and Cessation of Perception

“Therein, Poṭṭhapāda, those ascetics and brahmins who said: ‘Without cause and without condition, a person’s perceptions arise and cease,’ are mistaken from the very start. For what reason? For with cause and with condition, Poṭṭhapāda, a person’s perceptions arise and cease. Through |training::systematic cultivation, discipline [sikkhā]|, one perception arises; through training, one perception ceases.

And what is that training?” the Blessed One said.

“Here, Poṭṭhapāda, a |Tathāgata::one who has arrived at the truth, an epithet of a perfectly Awakened One [tathāgata]|, an Arahant, a perfectly Awakened One arises in the world ... In this way, Poṭṭhapāda, a bhikkhu is |accomplished in virtue::of excellent morality; accomplished in ethical conduct [sīlasampanna]|.

When he sees that these |five hindrances::the five mental obstacles—sensual desire, ill will, dullness and drowsiness, restlessness and worry, and doubt—that block clarity and stillness [pañca nīvaraṇā]| have been abandoned within himself, |joy::cheerfulness, gladness; a fresh and mild happiness arising from a sense of spiritual well-being and a clear conscience [pāmojja]| arises. When one is joyful, |uplifting joy::mental exhilaration; it ranges from a gentle delight to overwhelming rapture [pīti]| arises. In one whose mind is filled with uplifting joy, the body |becomes tranquil::calms down, relaxes [passambhati]|. One tranquil in body dwells in |contentment::ease, happiness, pleasant abiding [sukha]|. The mind of one who dwells in contentment |becomes collected::is calmed, becomes composed, becomes stable [samādhiyati]|.

Entirely secluded from sensual pleasures and |unwholesome::unhealthy, unskillful, unbeneficial, or karmically unprofitable [akusala]| mental states, he enters and dwells in the |first jhāna::the first meditative absorption, characterized by applied thought, sustained thought, and the joy and pleasure born of seclusion [paṭhamaṁ jhānaṁ]|, which is |accompanied by reflection::with thinking [savitakka]| and |examination::with investigation, evaluation [savicāra]|, |born of seclusion::secluded from the defilements [vivekaja]|, and imbued with |uplifting joy and pleasure::delight and ease; sometimes experienced as ecstasy, intense exhilaration or rapture [pītisukha]|. Whatever perception of sensuality he had previously ceases. At that time, there is a subtle but actual perception of uplifting joy and pleasure born of seclusion, and at that time, he is one who perceives the subtle but actual uplifting joy and pleasure born of seclusion. In this way too, through training, one perception arises and through training, one perception ceases. This is the training,” the Blessed One said.

“Furthermore, Poṭṭhapāda, with the |settling::calming, conciliation, subsiding [vūpasama]| of reflection and examination, a bhikkhu enters and dwells in the |second jhāna::the second meditative absorption, characterized by inner confidence and unification of mind, free from thinking and examining, with joy and pleasure born of collectedness [dutiyaṁ jhānaṁ]|, which has |inner confidence::internal tranquility, clarifying of mind [sampasādana]| and |unification::singleness, integration [ekodibhāva]| of mind, is without reflection and examining, and has joy and pleasure |born of collectedness::born from a stable mind [samādhija]|. Whatever subtle but actual perception of uplifting joy and pleasure born of seclusion he had previously ceases. At that time, there is a subtle but actual perception of uplifting joy and pleasure born of collectedness, and at that time, he is one who perceives the subtle but actual uplifting joy and pleasure born of collectedness. In this way too, through training, one perception arises and through training, one perception ceases. This too is the training,” the Blessed One said.

“Furthermore, Poṭṭhapāda, with the |fading of desire for::dispassion toward, detachment from [virāga]| uplifting joy, the bhikkhu dwells |equanimous::mental poised, mentally balanced, non-reactive, disregarding [upekkhaka]|, |mindful and clearly aware::attentive and completely comprehending [sata + sampajāna]|, experiencing |pleasure::comfort, contentedness, happiness, ease [sukha]| with the body. He enters and dwells in the |third jhāna::the third meditative absorption of which the noble ones declare 'one who is equanimous and mindful dwells in pleasure' [tatiyaṁ jhānaṁ]|, of which the Noble Ones describe as, ‘one who dwells equanimous, mindful, and at ease.’ Whatever subtle but actual perception of uplifting joy and pleasure born of collectedness he had previously ceases. At that time, there is a subtle but actual perception of equanimous pleasure, and at that time, he is one who perceives the subtle but actual equanimous pleasure. In this way too, through training, one perception arises and through training, one perception ceases. This too is the training,” the Blessed One said.

“Furthermore, Poṭṭhapāda, with the abandoning of [bodily] pleasure and pain, and with the prior settling down of |mental pleasure and displeasure::the duality of positive and negative states of mind; mental happiness and mental pain [somanassadomanassa]|, a bhikkhu enters and dwells in the |fourth jhāna::the fourth meditative absorption, characterized by neither-pain-nor-pleasure and the purity of mindfulness due to equanimity [catutthaṁ jhānaṁ]|, which is characterized by purification of mindfulness through equanimity, experiencing a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. Whatever subtle but actual perception of equanimous pleasure he had previously ceases. At that time, there is a subtle but actual perception of neither-pain-nor-pleasure, and at that time, he is one who perceives the subtle but actual neither-pain-nor-pleasure. In this way too, through training, one perception arises and through training, one perception ceases. This too is the training,” the Blessed One said.

“Furthermore, Poṭṭhapāda, with the complete surpassing of |perceptions of form::recognition of matter, concept of materiality [rūpasaññā]|, with the disappearance of |perception of sensory impact::recognition of sense impression, aversion; lit. striking against perception [paṭighasaññā]|, and non-attention to |perception of diversity::recognition of diversity, perception of multiplicity [nānattasaññā]|, aware that ‘space is boundless,’ one enters and dwells in the |base of boundless space::field of boundless expanse, dimension of infinite space [ākāsānañcāyatana]|. Whatever perception of form he had previously ceases. At that time, there is a subtle but actual perception of the base of boundless space, and at that time, he is one who perceives the subtle but actual base of boundless space. In this way too, through training, one perception arises and through training, one perception ceases. This too is the training,” the Blessed One said.

“Furthermore, Poṭṭhapāda, with the complete surpassing of the base of boundless space, aware that ‘consciousness is boundless,’ one enters and dwells in the |base of boundless consciousness::field of limitless awareness [viññāṇañcāyatana]|. Whatever subtle but actual perception of the base of boundless space he had previously ceases. At that time, there is a subtle but actual perception of the base of boundless consciousness, and at that time, he is one who perceives the subtle but actual base of boundless consciousness. In this way too, through training, one perception arises and through training, one perception ceases. This too is the training,” the Blessed One said.

“Furthermore, Poṭṭhapāda, with the complete surpassing of the base of boundless consciousness, aware that ‘there is nothing,’ one enters and dwells in the |base of nothingness::field of awareness centered on the absence of any distinct “something\" to grasp or hold onto [ākiñcaññāyatana]|. Whatever subtle but actual perception of the base of boundless consciousness he had previously ceases. At that time, there is a subtle but actual perception of the base of nothingness, and at that time, he is one who perceives the subtle but actual base of nothingness. In this way too, through training, one perception arises and through training, one perception ceases. This too is the training,” the Blessed One said.

“When, Poṭṭhapāda, a bhikkhu is here self-aware, from there to the next and from there to the next, he gradually touches the |culmination of perception::the peak of perception, the apex of percipience [saññagga]|. As he stands at the culmination of perception, it occurs to him: ‘|Intending::having mental activity [cetayamāna]| is worse for me; not intending is better for me. If I were to intend and |construct::formulate, fabricate, fashion, generate [abhisaṅkharoti]|, these perceptions would |cease::vanish, reach an end [nirujjhati]| in me and other coarser perceptions would arise. Why don’t I neither intend nor construct?’ He neither intends nor constructs. When he neither intends nor constructs, those very perceptions cease in him and other coarser perceptions do not arise. He touches |cessation::ending, termination [nirodha]|. In this way, Poṭṭhapāda, there is the |gradual attainment of the ultimate cessation of perception while clearly aware::attainment of the gradual total cessation of consciousness while clearly aware [anupubbābhisaññānirodhasampajānasamāpatti]|.

What do you think, Poṭṭhapāda, have you ever before heard of such a gradual attainment of the ultimate cessation of perception while clearly aware?”

“No, venerable sir. Venerable sir, I thus understand the statement of the Blessed One: ‘When, Poṭṭhapāda, a bhikkhu is here self-aware, from there to the next and from there to the next, he gradually touches the culmination of perception. As he stands at the culmination of perception, it occurs to him: “Intending is worse for me; not intending is better for me. If I were to intend and construct, these perceptions would cease in me and other coarser perceptions would arise. Why don’t I neither intend nor construct?” He neither intends nor constructs. When he neither intends nor constructs, those very perceptions cease in him and other coarser perceptions do not arise. He touches cessation. In this way, Poṭṭhapāda, there is the gradual attainment of the ultimate cessation of perception while clearly aware.’”

“Just so, Poṭṭhapāda.”

“Does the Blessed One, venerable sir, describe only a single culmination of perception, or does he describe many culminations of perception?”

“Poṭṭhapāda, I describe a single culmination of perception, and I also describe many culminations of perception.”

“In what way, venerable sir, does the Blessed One describe a single culmination of perception and also describe many culminations of perception?”

“According to how one touches cessation, Poṭṭhapāda, in that way do I describe the culmination of perception. In this way, Poṭṭhapāda, I describe a single culmination of perception, and I also describe many culminations of perception.”

“Does perception, venerable sir, arise first and knowledge afterwards, or does knowledge arise first and perception afterwards, or do perception and knowledge arise simultaneously, neither before nor after?”

“Perception, Poṭṭhapāda, arises first and |knowledge::understanding, direct comprehension [ñāṇa]| afterwards; through the arising of perception, there is the arising of knowledge. One discerns: ‘It was dependent on this condition that knowledge arose in me.’ In this way, Poṭṭhapāda, it should be understood how perception arises first and knowledge afterwards, and how through the arising of perception there is the arising of knowledge.”

#### 1.3. Perception and the Self

“Is perception, venerable sir, a person’s self, or is perception one thing and the self another?”

“Poṭṭhapāda, what kind of self do you postulate?”

“Venerable sir, I postulate a |coarse self::gross physical self [oḷārika attā]|, possessed of form, composed of the four great elements, and feeding on solid food.”

“Even if there were for you, Poṭṭhapāda, a coarse self, possessed of form, composed of the four great elements, and feeding on solid food—even so, Poṭṭhapāda, perception would still be one thing and the self another. In this way too, Poṭṭhapāda, it should be understood how perception would be one thing and the self another: while this coarse self, possessed of form, composed of the four great elements, and feeding on solid food remains, different perceptions arise in this person and different perceptions cease. In this way, Poṭṭhapāda, it should be understood how perception would be one thing and the self another.”

“Venerable sir, I postulate a |mind-made self::self composed of subtle mental form [manomaya attā]|, complete with all limbs and parts, lacking in no faculty.”

“Even if there were for you, Poṭṭhapāda, a mind-made self, complete with all limbs and parts, lacking in no faculty—even so, Poṭṭhapāda, perception would still be one thing and the self another. In this way too, Poṭṭhapāda, it should be understood how perception would be one thing and the self another: while this mind-made self, complete with all limbs and parts, lacking in no faculty remains, different perceptions arise in this person and different perceptions cease. In this way too, Poṭṭhapāda, it should be understood how perception would be one thing and the self another.”

“Venerable sir, I postulate a |formless self::an immaterial self [arūpī attā]|, |made of awareness::consisting of perception [saññāmaya]|.”

“Even if there were for you, Poṭṭhapāda, a formless self made of awareness—even so, Poṭṭhapāda, perception would still be one thing and the self another. In this way too, Poṭṭhapāda, it should be understood how perception would be one thing and the self another: while this formless self made of awareness remains, different perceptions arise in this person and different perceptions cease. In this way too, Poṭṭhapāda, it should be understood how perception would be one thing and the self another.”

“Is it possible for me, venerable sir, to know whether: ‘Perception is a person’s self,’ or ‘Perception is one thing and the self another’?”

“It is hard for you to know this, Poṭṭhapāda—holding other views, having other inclinations, having other preferences, following another pursuit, under another teacher—whether ‘Perception is a person’s self,’ or ‘Perception is one thing and the self another.’”

“If, venerable sir, it is hard for me to know—holding other views, having other inclinations, having other preferences, following another pursuit, under another teacher—whether ‘Perception is a person’s self,’ or ‘Perception is one thing and the self another;’ how then, venerable sir: ‘Is the world |eternal::everlasting [sassata]|; only this is true, anything else is wrong’?”

“Poṭṭhapāda, I have left this |unexplained::not stated, undeclared [abyākata]|: ‘The world is eternal; only this is true, anything else is wrong.’”

“How then, venerable sir: ‘Is the world not eternal; only this is true, anything else is wrong’?”

“This too, Poṭṭhapāda, I have left unexplained: ‘The world is not eternal; only this is true, anything else is wrong.’”

“How then, venerable sir: ‘Is the world finite ... Is the world infinite ... Are the |life force::life, soul [jīva]| and the body the same ... Is the life force one thing and the body another ... After death, does a Tathāgata exist ... After death, does a Tathāgata not exist ... After death, does a Tathāgata both exist and not exist ... After death, does a Tathāgata neither exist nor not exist; only this is true, anything else is wrong’?”

“This too, Poṭṭhapāda, I have left unexplained: ‘After death, a Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist; only this is true, anything else is wrong.’”

“Why, venerable sir, has this been left unexplained by the Blessed One?”

“Because, Poṭṭhapāda, this is not beneficial, not connected with the |Dhamma::the ultimate truth that the Buddha’s teachings point to [dhamma]|, not fundamental to the |spiritual life::a life of celibacy, contemplation, and ethical discipline lived for the sake of liberation; oriented toward inner development rather than sensual pleasures [brahmacariya]|; it does not lead to |disenchantment::de-illusionment, disinterest, dispassion [nibbidā]|, to the |fading of desire::dispassion, detachment [virāga]|, to |cessation::ending, termination [nirodha]|, to |tranquility::calmness, serenity, stillness, peace [upasama]|, to |direct knowledge::experiential understanding [abhiññā]|, to |full awakening::perfect understanding, enlightenment [sambodha]|, to |Nibbāna::complete cooling, letting go of everything, deathless, freedom from calamity, the non-disintegrating; lit. blowing away [nibbāna]|. That is why I have left it unexplained.”

“And what, venerable sir, has been explained by the Blessed One?”

“‘This is |suffering::discomfort, unpleasantness, discontentment, dissatisfaction, stress, pain, disease, i.e. mild or intense suffering [dukkha]|’—this, Poṭṭhapāda, I have explained. ‘This is the |arising of suffering::source of stress, appearance of discomfort [dukkhasamudaya]|’—this, Poṭṭhapāda, I have explained. ‘This is the |cessation of suffering::end of suffering, cessation of distress [dukkhanirodha]|’—this, Poṭṭhapāda, I have explained. ‘This is the |way of practice leading to the cessation of suffering::i.e. the Noble Eightfold Path consisting of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right collectedness [dukkhanirodhagāminī paṭipadā]|’—this, Poṭṭhapāda, I have explained.”

“And why, venerable sir, has this been explained by the Blessed One?”

“Because, Poṭṭhapāda, this is beneficial, this is connected with the Dhamma, this is fundamental to the spiritual life; this leads to disenchantment, to the fading of desire, to cessation, to tranquility, to direct knowledge, to full awakening, to Nibbāna. That is why I have explained it.”

“So it is, Blessed One! So it is, Accomplished One! Now, venerable sir, may the Blessed One go at his convenience.” Then the Blessed One rose from his seat and departed.

Then, shortly after the Blessed One had departed, those wanderers overwhelmed the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda from all sides with barbed words, like striking him with goads: “Just so does sir Poṭṭhapāda applaud whatever the ascetic Gotama says: ‘So it is, Blessed One! So it is, Accomplished One!’ Yet we do not understand the ascetic Gotama as having taught any |definitive teaching::categorical, unequivocal doctrine [ekaṁsika dhamma]|: whether ‘The world is eternal,’ or ‘The world is not eternal,’ or ‘The world is finite,’ or ‘The world is infinite,’ or ‘The life force and the body are the same,’ or ‘The life force is one thing and the body another,’ or ‘After death, a Tathāgata exists,’ or ‘After death, a Tathāgata does not exist,’ or ‘After death, a Tathāgata both exists and does not exist,’ or ‘After death, a Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist.’”

When this was said, the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda said to those wanderers: “Sirs, I too do not understand the ascetic Gotama as having taught any definitive teaching: whether ‘The world is eternal,’ or ‘The world is not eternal’ ... or ‘After death, a Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist.’ But the ascetic Gotama describes a practice that is actual, real, and true, grounded in the |regularity of natural principles::stability of the Dhamma [dhammaṭṭhitatā]|, the |invariance of natural principles::orderliness of the teaching [dhammaniyāmatā]|. When he describes a practice that is actual, real, and true, grounded in the regularity of natural principles and the invariance of natural principles, how could a discerning person like me not applaud what was well spoken by the ascetic Gotama as well spoken?”

### 2. Citta Hatthisāriputta and Poṭṭhapāda

Then, after the passage of two or three days, |Citta::::Chitta| Hatthisāriputta and the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda approached the Blessed One. Having approached, |Citta::::Chitta| Hatthisāriputta paid homage to the Blessed One and sat down to one side. The wanderer Poṭṭhapāda, however, exchanged friendly greetings with the Blessed One. After exchanging courteous and polite conversation, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the wanderer Poṭṭhapāda said to the Blessed One: “At that time, venerable sir, shortly after the Blessed One had departed, those wanderers overwhelmed me from all sides with barbed words, like striking me with goads: ‘Just so does sir Poṭṭhapāda applaud whatever the ascetic Gotama says: “So it is, Blessed One! So it is, Accomplished One!” Yet we do not understand the ascetic Gotama as having taught any definitive teaching: whether “The world is eternal,” or “The world is not eternal,” or “The world is finite,” or “The world is infinite,” or “The life force and the body are the same,” or “The life force is one thing and the body another,” or “After death, a Tathāgata exists,” or “After death, a Tathāgata does not exist,” or “After death, a Tathāgata both exists and does not exist,” or “After death, a Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist.”’ When this was said, venerable sir, I said to those wanderers: ‘Sirs, I too do not understand the ascetic Gotama as having taught any definitive teaching: whether “The world is eternal,” or “The world is not eternal” ... or “After death, a Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist.” But the ascetic Gotama describes a practice that is actual, real, and true, grounded in the regularity of natural principles, the invariance of natural principles. When he describes a practice that is actual, real, and true, grounded in the regularity of natural principles and the invariance of natural principles, how could a discerning person like me not applaud what was well spoken by the ascetic Gotama as well spoken?’”

“All those wanderers, Poṭṭhapāda, are blind and without vision; you alone among them have vision. For I have taught and declared |definitive teachings::categorical, unequivocal teachings [ekaṁsikā dhammā]|, Poṭṭhapāda, and I have also declared |matters that are non-definitive::non-categorical, indefinite topics or views [anekaṁsikā dhammā]|.

And what, Poṭṭhapāda, are the matters I have declared to be non-definitive? ‘The world is eternal’ is a matter I have declared to be non-definitive. ‘The world is not eternal’ is a matter I have declared to be non-definitive. ‘The world is finite’ ... ‘The world is infinite’ ... ‘The life force and the body are the same’ ... ‘The life force is one thing and the body another’ ... ‘After death, a Tathāgata exists’ ... ‘After death, a Tathāgata does not exist’ ... ‘After death, a Tathāgata both exists and does not exist’ ... ‘After death, a Tathāgata neither exists nor does not exist’ is a matter I have declared to be non-definitive.

And why, Poṭṭhapāda, have I declared these to be non-definitive? Because they are not beneficial, not connected with the Dhamma, not fundamental to the spiritual life; they do not lead to disenchantment, to the fading of desire, to cessation, to tranquility, to direct knowledge, to full awakening, to Nibbāna. That is why I have declared them to be non-definitive.

#### 2.1. Definitive Teachings

And what, Poṭṭhapāda, are the teachings I have declared to be definitive? ‘This is suffering’ is a teaching I have declared to be definitive. ‘This is the arising of suffering’ is a teaching I have declared to be definitive. ‘This is the cessation of suffering’ is a teaching I have declared to be definitive. ‘This is the way of practice leading to the cessation of suffering’ is a teaching I have declared to be definitive.

And why, Poṭṭhapāda, have I declared these to be definitive? Because they are beneficial, they are connected with the Dhamma, they are fundamental to the spiritual life; they lead to disenchantment, to the fading of desire, to cessation, to tranquility, to direct knowledge, to full awakening, to Nibbāna. That is why I have declared them to be definitive.

#### 2.3. The Three Acquisitions of Self
