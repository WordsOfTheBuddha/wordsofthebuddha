Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was on a walking tour, wandering in the |Malla::name of the people of Malla; Mallans; lit. wrestlers [malla]| country with a large |Saṅgha::The community of monks and nuns practicing in line with the Buddha’s teachings. In the broader sense, this is the community of disciples who have realized the noble path and fruition through the Buddha’s teachings [saṅgha]| of five hundred bhikkhus, and eventually he arrived at a Mallan city named |Pāvā::name of a city of the Mallas [pāvā]|. There, the Blessed One stayed at Pāvā in the mango grove of Cunda the smith.

Now at that time, a new assembly hall named Ubbhataka had recently been built by the Mallas of Pāvā, which had not yet been occupied by any ascetic, brahmin, or human being. The Mallas of Pāvā heard: “The Blessed One, it seems, while wandering on a walking tour among the Mallas with a large Saṅgha of five hundred bhikkhus, has arrived at Pāvā and is staying at Pāvā in the mango grove of Cunda the smith.” Then the Mallas of Pāvā approached the Blessed One; having approached, they paid homage to the Blessed One and sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the Mallas of Pāvā said to the Blessed One: “Here, venerable sir, a new assembly hall named Ubbhataka has recently been built by the Mallas of Pāvā, which has not yet been occupied by any ascetic, brahmin, or human being. Venerable sir, may the Blessed One use it first. After the Blessed One has used it first, the Mallas of Pāvā will use it. That will be for the welfare and happiness of the Mallas of Pāvā for a long time.” The Blessed One consented in silence.

Then the Mallas of Pāvā, having understood the Blessed One’s consent, rose from their seats, and after paying homage to the Blessed One, keeping him on their right, approached the assembly hall. Having approached, they covered the entire assembly hall with mats, prepared seats for the Blessed One, set out a water jar, and hung up an oil lamp. Then they approached the Blessed One; having approached and paid homage to the Blessed One, they stood to one side. Standing to one side, those Mallas of Pāvā said to the Blessed One: “Venerable sir, the assembly hall is completely covered with mats, seats are prepared for the Blessed One, a water jar is set out, and an oil lamp is hung up. Venerable sir, may the Blessed One come at his own convenience.”

Then the Blessed One, after putting on his robe and taking his alms bowl and outer robe, approached the assembly hall together with the Saṅgha of bhikkhus. Having approached, washed his feet, and entered the assembly hall, he sat down against the middle pillar, facing east. The Saṅgha of bhikkhus also, having washed their feet and entered the assembly hall, sat down against the western wall, facing east, keeping the Blessed One in front of them. The Mallas of Pāvā also, having washed their feet and entered the assembly hall, sat down against the eastern wall, facing west, keeping the Blessed One in front of them.

Then the Blessed One, having instructed, encouraged, inspired, and gladdened the Mallas of Pāvā for much of the night with a talk on the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]|, dismissed them, saying: “The night has advanced, Vāseṭṭhas. Now do whatever you think it is time for.”

“Yes, venerable sir,” the Mallas of Pāvā replied to the Blessed One. They rose from their seats, and after paying homage to the Blessed One, keeping him on their right, departed.

Then, not long after the Mallas of Pāvā had departed, the Blessed One surveyed the completely silent Saṅgha of bhikkhus and addressed the venerable |Sāriputta::foremost disciple of the Buddha in great wisdom [sāriputta]|: “The Saṅgha of bhikkhus is free from |dullness and drowsiness::lack of mental clarity or alertness, mental sluggishness, lethargy, sleepiness; lit. stiffness (of mind/body due to tiredness) [thinamiddha]|, Sāriputta. Let a talk on the Dhamma occur to you for the bhikkhus, Sāriputta. My back |aches::is sore, is tired [āgilāyati]|; I will stretch it.”

“Yes, venerable sir,” the venerable Sāriputta replied to the Blessed One.

Then the Blessed One, having folded his outer robe in four, lay down on his right side in the lion’s posture, placing one foot overlapping the other, |mindful::having active presence of mind and lucid retention; remaining naturally anchored and attentive without losing one's bearings [sata]| and |clearly aware::with attentiveness, with clear and full comprehension, intentional, deliberate, purposeful [sampajāna]|, having attended to the perception of rising up.

Now at that time, |Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta::the Jain leader Mahāvīra; lit. the knotless one, son of the Nāṭa clan [nigaṇṭha nāṭaputta]| had recently passed away at Pāvā. Following his death, the Nigaṇṭhas were split, divided into two factions, engaging in blaming, quarreling, and disputing with one another, stabbing each other with pointed words: “You do not understand this doctrine and discipline; I understand this doctrine and discipline! How will you understand this doctrine and discipline? You are practicing wrongly; I am practicing rightly! What I say is consistent; what you say is inconsistent! What should be said first you said last; what should be said last you said first! What you have long practiced has been turned upside down; your doctrine has been refuted; you are defeated! Go and save your doctrine, or disentangle yourself if you can!”

It seemed as though there was nothing but slaughter going on among the followers of Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta. Even the white-clothed lay disciples of Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta were disillusioned, dismayed, and repelled by the followers of Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta, just as they were with that poorly declared doctrine and discipline, which is poorly proclaimed, not leading to deliverance, not conducive to tranquility, not made known by a perfectly Awakened One, with its shrine broken, left without a refuge.

Then the venerable Sāriputta addressed the bhikkhus: “Friends, Nigaṇṭha Nāṭaputta has recently passed away at Pāvā. Following his death, the Nigaṇṭhas have split, divided into two factions ... with its shrine broken, left without a refuge. For that is how it is, friends, when a doctrine and discipline is poorly declared, poorly proclaimed, not leading to deliverance, not conducive to tranquility, and not made known by a perfectly Awakened One. But this Dhamma, friends, has been well proclaimed by our Blessed One, well expounded, |leading to liberation::redemptive, leading to deliverance [niyyānika]|, conducive to |tranquility::calmness, serenity, stillness, peace [upasama]|, and made known by a perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should |recite in concert::chant together in harmony, rehearse communally [saṅgāyitabba]| and not dispute, so that this |spiritual life::a life of celibacy, contemplation, and ethical discipline lived for the sake of liberation; oriented toward inner development rather than sensual pleasures [brahmacariya]| may last long and endure for a long time. That will be for the welfare of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.

And what, friends, is the Dhamma that has been well proclaimed by our Blessed One, well expounded, leading to liberation, conducive to tranquility, and made known by a perfectly Awakened One, which all should recite in concert and not dispute, so that this spiritual life may last long and endure for a long time, for the welfare of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans?

#### 1. The Ones

There is, friends, one principle rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the |Arahant::a worthy one, a fully awakened being, epithet of the Buddha [arahant]|, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert and not dispute, so that this spiritual life may last long and endure for a long time, for the welfare of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What one principle?

All sentient beings are sustained by |nutriment::food, fuel, nourishment, sustenance [āhāra]|.

All sentient beings are sustained by |conditions::intentional constructs, formations, conditioning processes [saṅkhārā]|.

This, friends, is the one principle rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert and not dispute, so that this spiritual life may last long and endure for a long time, for the welfare of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.

#### 3. The Threes

There are, friends, triads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What triads?

Three roots of the unwholesome—|greed::grasping craving, possessiveness, lustful wanting [lobha]| is a root of the unwholesome, |aversion::hatred, hostility, ill will [dosa]| is a root of the unwholesome, and |delusion::confusion, misapprehension of reality [moha]| is a root of the unwholesome.

Three roots of the wholesome—|non-greed::absence of craving; contentment, generosity, renunciation [alobha]| is a root of the wholesome, |non-aversion::absence of ill will; loving-kindness, goodwill [adosa]| is a root of the wholesome, and |non-delusion::absence of confusion; wisdom, clear understanding [amoha]| is a root of the wholesome.

Three kinds of misconduct—bodily misconduct, verbal misconduct, and mental misconduct.

Three kinds of good conduct—bodily good conduct, verbal good conduct, and mental good conduct.

Three unwholesome thoughts—thought of sensuality, thought of ill will, and thought of harming.

Three wholesome thoughts—thought of |renunciation::letting go of sensual pleasures [nekkhamma]|, thought of non-ill will, and thought of harmlessness.

Three unwholesome intentions—intention of sensuality, intention of ill will, and intention of harming.

Three wholesome intentions—intention of renunciation, intention of non-ill will, and intention of harmlessness.

Three unwholesome perceptions—perception of sensuality, perception of ill will, and perception of harming.

Three wholesome perceptions—perception of renunciation, perception of non-ill will, and perception of harmlessness.

Three unwholesome elements—the element of sensuality, the element of ill will, and the element of harming.

Three wholesome elements—the element of renunciation, the element of non-ill will, and the element of harmlessness.

Another three elements—the sensuality element, the |form element::objects of vision, materials, including the realm and objects of subtle materiality [rūpadhātu]|, and the |formless element::states not rooted in materiality, including mental realms and meditative attainments beyond physical form [arūpadhātu]|.

Another three elements—the form element, the formless element, and the |element of cessation::the complete ending of conditioned phenomena, including the cessation of perception and what is felt, leading to Nibbāna [nirodhadhātu]|.

Another three elements—the inferior state, the middling state, and the sublime state.

Three kinds of craving—|craving for sensual pleasures::desire specifically for objects of the five senses [kāmataṇhā]|, |craving for existence::craving for continued existence, desire for a stable identity, attachment to a becoming, a future self, attainment, or experience [bhavataṇhā]|, and |craving for non-existence::desire for annihilation, for ceasing to be [vibhavataṇhā]|.

Another three kinds of craving—craving for sensual pleasures, craving for form, and craving for the formless.

Another three kinds of craving—craving for form, craving for the formless, and craving for cessation.

Three fetters—|personal existence view::the view of being someone; regarding any of the five aggregates as a self [sakkāyadiṭṭhi]|, |doubt::uncertainty, indecisiveness regarding the Teacher, Dhamma, Saṅgha, and training [vicikicchā]|, and |clinging to rules and observances::adherence to rites and rituals as sufficient for liberation [sīlabbataparāmāsa]|.

Three taints—the |taint of sensual desire::oozing sensual desire [kāmāsava]|, the |taint of becoming::effluent of becoming, taint of existence [bhavāsava]|, and the |taint of ignorance::taint of not knowing how things have come to be [avijjāsava]|.

Three kinds of existence—|existence in the realm of sense desire::sensual existence [kāmabhava]|, |existence in the realm of form::existence in which beings experience a refined state of form without sensual desire [rūpabhava]|, and |existence in the formless realm::existence characterized by the absence of material form [arūpabhava]|.

Three |quests::searches, pursuits [esanā]|—the quest for sensual pleasure, the quest for existence, and the quest for spiritual life.

Three modes of |conceit::self-measurement, comparative pride [vidhā]|—the conceit ‘I am superior,’ the conceit ‘I am equal,’ and the conceit ‘I am inferior.’

Three time periods—the past time period, the future time period, and the present time period.

Three |extremes::limits, boundaries of self-identification [antā]|—personal existence as an extreme, the arising of personal existence as an extreme, and the cessation of personal existence as an extreme.

Three feelings—pleasant feeling, painful feeling, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling.

Three kinds of |suffering::discomfort, unpleasantness, discontentment, dissatisfaction, stress, pain, disease, i.e. mild or intense suffering [dukkha]|—the ordinary suffering, the suffering due to |conditions::constructed phenomena requiring continual maintenance [saṅkhāradukkhatā]|, and the suffering due to change.

Three |heaps::piles, collections [rāsī]|—the heap fixed in wrongness, the heap fixed in rightness, and the uncertain heap.

Three |obscurations::darknesses of mind [tamā]|—concerning the past time period, one has doubt and uncertainty, is undecided, and lacks clarity; concerning the future time period, one has doubt and uncertainty, is undecided, and lacks clarity; or concerning the present time period now, one has doubt and uncertainty, is undecided, and lacks clarity.

Three things a |Tathāgata::one who has arrived at the truth, an epithet of a perfectly Awakened One [tathāgata]| has no need to guard—a Tathāgata, friends, is pure in bodily conduct; there is no bodily misconduct in the Tathāgata that the Tathāgata would need to guard, thinking: ‘Let others not know this about me.’ A Tathāgata, friends, is pure in verbal conduct; there is no verbal misconduct in the Tathāgata that the Tathāgata would need to guard, thinking: ‘Let others not know this about me.’ A Tathāgata, friends, is pure in mental conduct; there is no mental misconduct in the Tathāgata that the Tathāgata would need to guard, thinking: ‘Let others not know this about me.’

Three |obstructions::attachments, inward obstacles; lit. something [kiñcanā]|—passion is an obstruction, aversion is an obstruction, and delusion is an obstruction.

Three fires—the fire of passion, the fire of aversion, and the fire of delusion.

Another three fires—the fire of those worthy of offerings, the householder’s fire, and the fire of those worthy of gifts.

Threefold classification of form—form that is visible and impinging, form that is invisible and impinging, and form that is invisible and non-impinging.

Three |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]|—the meritorious intentional construct, the demeritorious intentional construct, and the |imperturbable::unshakable, corresponding to the formless attainments [āneñja]| intentional construct.

Three persons—a |trainee::disciple in higher training who has entered the noble path but not yet reached arahantship [sekkha]|, an |adept::one beyond training, an arahant [asekkha]|, and one who is neither a trainee nor an adept.

Three elders—an elder by birth, an elder according to the Dhamma, and an elder by convention.

Three bases for making merit—the basis for making merit through |giving::generosity, donating [dāna]|, the basis for making merit through |ethical conduct::virtue, moral integrity [sīla]|, and the basis for making merit through |cultivation::meditative development [bhāvanā]|.

Three grounds for |criticizing::bringing an accusation or admonition [codanā]|—based on what has been seen, based on what has been heard, and based on what is suspected.

Three kinds of rebirth in the sensual realm—there are, friends, sentient beings whose sensual pleasures are presently available, and who exercise mastery over presently available sensual pleasures, such as humans, certain gods, and some who have fallen into a |realm of downfall::realm of misery [vinipāta]|. This is the first kind of rebirth in the sensual realm. There are, friends, sentient beings who delight in creating sensual pleasures, and who, having created them again and again, exercise mastery over sensual pleasures, such as the |gods who delight in creation::deities of the Nimmānarati heaven [devā nimmānaratī]|. This is the second kind of rebirth in the sensual realm. There are, friends, sentient beings whose sensual pleasures are created by others, and who exercise mastery over sensual pleasures created by others, such as the |gods who wield power over others’ creations::deities of the Paranimmitavasavatti heaven [devā paranimmitavasavattī]|. This is the third kind of rebirth in the sensual realm.

Three kinds of rebirth in pleasure—there are, friends, sentient beings who, having generated pleasure again and again, dwell in pleasure, such as the gods of Brahmā’s assembly. This is the first kind of rebirth in pleasure. There are, friends, sentient beings who are drenched, steeped, filled, and suffused with pleasure; from time to time they utter the exclamation: ‘Oh, what bliss! Oh, what bliss!’—such as the |radiant gods::effulgent deities of the second jhāna plane [devā ābhassarā]|. This is the second kind of rebirth in pleasure. There are, friends, sentient beings who are drenched, steeped, filled, and suffused with pleasure; contented, they experience only peaceful pleasure, such as the |gods of Refulgent Glory::deities of the third jhāna plane [devā subhakiṇhā]|. This is the third kind of rebirth in pleasure.

Three kinds of wisdom—the wisdom of a trainee, the wisdom of an adept, and the wisdom of one who is neither a trainee nor an adept.

Another three kinds of wisdom—wisdom based on reflection, wisdom based on learning, and wisdom based on cultivation.

Three weapons—the weapon of learning, the weapon of |seclusion::detachment, solitude [paviveka]|, and the weapon of wisdom.

Three faculties—the faculty of ‘I shall come to know the unknown,’ the faculty of final knowledge, and the faculty of one who is endowed with final knowledge.

Three eyes—the physical eye, the divine eye, and the eye of wisdom.

Three trainings—the training in higher virtue, the training in higher mind, and the training in higher wisdom.

Three kinds of cultivation—the cultivation of the body, the cultivation of the mind, and the cultivation of wisdom.

Three unsurpassable things—the unsurpassable in vision, the unsurpassable in way of practice, and the unsurpassable in liberation.

Three kinds of collectedness—collectedness accompanied by |thought::initial application of mind, reflection [vitakka]| and |examination::sustained application, investigation [vicāra]|, collectedness without thought and with a measure of examination, and collectedness without thought and without examination.

Another three kinds of collectedness—collectedness accompanied by |emptiness::devoid of self or what belongs to self [suññata]|, |signless::free from perceptual signs [animitta]| collectedness, and |undirected::desireless, free from longing [appaṇihita]| collectedness.

Three kinds of purity—bodily purity, verbal purity, and mental purity.

Three kinds of |sagacity::sagehood, silent wisdom [moneyya]|—bodily sagacity, verbal sagacity, and mental sagacity.

Three kinds of skill—skill in |progress::growth, gain [āya]|, skill in |decline::loss, ruin [apāya]|, and skill in |means::expedient method [upāya]|.

Three |intoxications::infatuations, vanities [madā]|—intoxication with health, intoxication with youth, and intoxication with life.

Three |authorities::determining factors, powers [ādhipateyya]|—taking oneself as one’s authority, taking the world as one’s authority, and taking the Dhamma as one’s authority.

Three bases of conversation—engaging in a conversation concerning the past, one might say: ‘It was like this in the past’; engaging in a conversation concerning the future, one might say: ‘It will be like this in the future’; or engaging in a conversation concerning the present now, one might say: ‘It is like this now in the present.’

Three |true knowledges::higher knowledges [vijjā]|—the knowledge of the recollection of past lives as a true knowledge, the knowledge of the passing away and rebirth of beings as a true knowledge, and the knowledge of the wearing away of the taints as a true knowledge.

Three abidings—the heavenly abiding, the |brahmic abiding::boundless loving-kindness, compassion, appreciative joy, and equanimity [brahmavihāra]|, and the noble abiding.

Three miracles—the miracle of psychic power, the miracle of |mind reading::lit. causing to point out [ādesanā]|, and the miracle of instruction.

These, friends, are the triads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.

#### 4. The Fours

There are, friends, tetrads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert and not dispute ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What tetrads?

Four |establishments of mindfulness::foundations or objective domains of mindfulness [satipaṭṭhānā]|. Here, friends, a bhikkhu dwells observing the body in and of itself, with continuous effort, clearly aware and with excellent recollection, having removed |craving and distress::wanting and unhappiness, greed and aversion [abhijjhādomanassa]| with regard to the world. He dwells observing the felt experience in and of itself ... observing the mind in and of itself ... observing |mental qualities::phenomena, principles, traits and tendencies of the mind [dhammā]| in and of themselves, with continuous effort, clearly aware and with excellent recollection, having removed craving and distress with regard to the world.

Four right efforts. Here, friends, a bhikkhu produces |a wholesome motivation::an aspiration, a goal, an interest, an objective [chanda]|, |exerts effort::engages in intentional effort [vāyamati]|, |initiates and sustains energy::the initial spark of motivation and action along with the persistence needed to follow through, even as challenges arise [vīriya + ārabhati]|, |uplifts::befriends and holds it up [paggaṇhāti]| the mind, and strives to prevent the arising of unarisen |harmful::injurious, bad, or evil. Sentient beings, friends, are reborn—with the breaking up of the body, after death—in a state of loss, in a bad destination, in a realm of downfall, in hell because of loss of ethical conduct or because of a disastrous view.

Sentient beings, friends, are not reborn—with the breaking up of the body, after death—in a good destination, in a heavenly world because of accomplishment in relatives, accomplishment in wealth, or accomplishment in health. Sentient beings, friends, are reborn—with the breaking up of the body, after death—in a good destination, in a heavenly world because of accomplishment in ethical conduct or because of the successful attainment of view.

Furthermore, friends, with the breaking up of the body, after death, a virtuous person who succeeds in ethical conduct is reborn in a good destination, in a heavenly world; this is the fifth benefit for a virtuous person who succeeds in ethical conduct.

When a bhikkhu who reproves, friends, wishes to reprove another, he should establish five qualities within himself before reproving the other: ‘I will speak at a proper time, not at an improper time; I will speak what is true, not what is false; I will speak gently, not harshly; I will speak what is beneficial, not what is not beneficial; I will speak with a mind of loving-kindness, not with |aversion::lit. with hatred inside [dosantarena]|.’ When a bhikkhu who reproves, friends, wishes to reprove another, he should establish these five qualities within himself before reproving the other.

Five factors of striving. Here, friends, a bhikkhu has faith; he places faith in the Tathāgata’s awakening: ‘The Blessed One is an Arahant, a perfectly Awakened One, accomplished in true knowledge and conduct, who has reached the destination, knower of the world, an unsurpassed guide of trainable persons, a teacher of gods and humans, Buddha, Fortunate One.’ He is free from illness and affliction, endowed with a balanced digestion that is neither too cold nor too hot, but moderate and suitable for striving. He is neither deceitful nor hypocritical, revealing himself as he truly is to the Teacher or to wise spiritual companions. He dwells with energy aroused for the abandoning of unwholesome qualities and the acquisition of wholesome qualities; he is |firm::steadfast, having stamina [thāmavant]|, |steadfast in striving::persistent in effort [daḷhaparakkama]|, and |never lays down the yoke::unwavering in commitment [anikkhittadhura]| with regard to wholesome qualities. And he is wise, endowed with wisdom that discerns arising and passing away, which is noble, penetrative, and leads to the complete wearing away of suffering.

Five |Pure Abodes::highest fine-material Brahma realms where only non-returners are reborn [suddhāvāsā]|—the Aviha gods, the Atappa gods, the Sudassa gods, the Sudassī gods, and the Akaniṭṭha gods.

When his mind does not incline toward effort, examination, perseverance, and striving, this is the fifth barrier of the mind.

Five faculties—the eye faculty, the ear faculty, the nose faculty, the tongue faculty, and the body faculty.

Another five faculties—the pleasure faculty, the pain faculty, the mental pleasure faculty, the mental displeasure faculty, and the equanimity faculty.

Another five faculties—the faith faculty, the energy faculty, the mindfulness faculty, the collectedness faculty, and the wisdom faculty.

Five |causes for liberation::occasions or avenues through which liberation is attained [vimuttāyatanāni]|. Here, friends, the Teacher or a spiritual companion standing in place of teacher teaches the Dhamma to a bhikkhu. In whatever way, friends, the Teacher or a spiritual companion standing in place of teacher teaches the Dhamma to the bhikkhu, in just that way he experiences the meaning and experiences the Dhamma. #### 6. The Sixes

There are, friends, hexads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One; herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What hexads?

Six internal sense bases—the eye base, the ear base, the nose base, the tongue base, the body base, and the mind base.

Six external sense bases—the form base, the sound base, the odor base, the taste base, the tangible object base, and the mental object base.

Six classes of consciousness—eye-consciousness, ear-consciousness, nose-consciousness, tongue-consciousness, body-consciousness, and mind-consciousness.

Six classes of contact—eye-contact, ear-contact, nose-contact, tongue-contact, body-contact, and mind-contact.

Six classes of perception—perception of form, perception of sound, perception of odor, perception of taste, perception of tangible object, and perception of mental object.

Six classes of intention—intention regarding form, intention regarding sound, intention regarding odor, intention regarding taste, intention regarding tangible object, and intention regarding mental object.

Six classes of craving—craving for form, craving for sound, craving for odor, craving for taste, craving for tangible object, and craving for mental object.

Six kinds of disrespect. Here, friends, a bhikkhu dwells without respect and |deference::regard, reverence [appaṭissa]| toward the Teacher; he dwells without respect and deference toward the Dhamma; he dwells without respect and deference toward the Saṅgha; he dwells without respect and deference toward the training; he dwells without respect and deference toward |diligence::heedfulness [appamāda]|; and he dwells without respect and deference toward hospitality.

having touched a tangible object with the body ... having cognized a mental object with the mind, one explores a mental object conducive to mental pleasure.

Six mental explorations accompanied by |mental displeasure::disagreeable feeling born of mental contact [domanassa]|. This too is a principle of cordiality ... and leads to unity.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu dwells—both openly and in private—possessing in common with his fellow spiritual companions those virtues that are unbroken, untorn, not spotty, unblemished, freeing, praised by the wise, irreproachable, and |leading to collectedness::leading to samādhi, conducive to mental composure [samādhisaṁvattanika]|. This too is a principle of cordiality ... and leads to unity.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu dwells—both openly and in private—possessing in common with his fellow spiritual companions that view which is noble and leading to liberation, and which leads one who practices it to the complete wearing away of suffering. This too is a principle of cordiality that creates affection and respect, and leads to cohesion, non-dispute, harmony, and unity.

Six roots of dispute. Here, friends, a bhikkhu is |irritable::prone to anger [kodhana]| and |resentful::bearing grudges [upanāhī]|. When a bhikkhu, friends, is irritable and resentful, he dwells without respect and deference toward the Teacher, dwells without respect and deference toward the Dhamma, dwells without respect and deference toward the Saṅgha, and does not fulfill the training. When a bhikkhu, friends, dwells without respect and deference toward the Teacher, dwells without respect and deference toward the Dhamma, dwells without respect and deference toward the Saṅgha, and does not fulfill the training, he instigates a dispute in the Saṅgha, which is for the harm of the many, for the unhappiness of the many, for the ruin, harm, and suffering of gods and humans. Thus there is the abandoning of this harmful root of dispute; thus there is the non-arising of this harmful root of dispute in the future.

When a bhikkhu, friends, clings to his own views, holding on tightly and relinquishing with difficulty, he dwells without respect and deference toward the Teacher, dwells without respect and deference toward the Dhamma, dwells without respect and deference toward the Saṅgha, and does not fulfill the training. When a bhikkhu, friends, dwells without respect and deference toward the Teacher, dwells without respect and deference toward the Dhamma, dwells without respect and deference toward the Saṅgha, and does not fulfill the training, he instigates a dispute in the Saṅgha, which is for the harm of the many, for the unhappiness of the many, for the ruin, harm, and suffering of gods and humans. Six elements—the earth element, the water element, the fire element, the wind element, the space element, and the consciousness element.

#### 7. The Sevens

There are, friends, heptads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One; herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What heptads?

Seven kinds of noble wealth—the wealth of faith, the wealth of virtue, the wealth of sense of right and wrong, the wealth of moral dread, the wealth of learning, the wealth of generosity, and the wealth of wisdom.

#### 8. The Eights

There are, friends, octads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One; herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What octads?

Eight wrong factors—|wrong view::distorted view contrary to reality [micchādiṭṭhi]|, |wrong intention::intention of sensuality, ill will, or harming [micchāsaṅkappa]|, |wrong speech::false, divisive, harsh, or frivolous speech [micchāvācā]|, |wrong action::killing, stealing, or sexual misconduct [micchākammanta]|, |wrong livelihood::deceitful or harmful means of living [micchāājīva]|, |wrong effort::effort directed toward unwholesome states [micchāvāyāma]|, |wrong mindfulness::misdirected attention reinforcing defilements [micchāsati]|, and |wrong collectedness::narrow fixation accompanied by defilements [micchāsamādhi]|.

Eight right factors—right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right collectedness.

Eight persons worthy of gifts—the stream-enterer and one practicing for the realization of the fruit of stream-entry; the once-returner and one practicing for the realization of the fruit of once-returning; the non-returner and one practicing for the realization of the fruit of non-returning; the arahant and one practicing for the realization of the fruit of arahantship.

Eight grounds of laziness. Here, friends, a bhikkhu has work to do. It occurs to him: ‘I will have work to do, and while I am doing work my body will get tired; let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy for the attainment of what has not yet been attained, for the achievement of what has not yet been achieved, and for the realization of what has not yet been realized. This is the first ground of laziness.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has done some work. It occurs to him: ‘I have done work, and while I was doing work my body got tired; let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy ... This is the second ground of laziness.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has a journey to travel. It occurs to him: ‘I will have a journey to travel, and while I am traveling the road my body will get tired; let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy ... This is the third ground of laziness.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has traveled a journey. It occurs to him: ‘I have traveled a journey, and while I was traveling the road my body got tired; let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy ... This is the fourth ground of laziness.

Furthermore, friends, while wandering for alms in a village or town, a bhikkhu does not get his fill of coarse or fine food. It occurs to him: ‘While wandering for alms in a village or town, I did not get my fill of coarse or fine food; my body is tired and unfit for work; let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy ... This is the fifth ground of laziness.

Furthermore, friends, while wandering for alms in a village or town, a bhikkhu gets his fill of coarse or fine food. It occurs to him: ‘While wandering for alms in a village or town, I got my fill of coarse or fine food; my body is heavy and unfit for work, like a heap of soaked beans; let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy ... This is the sixth ground of laziness.

Furthermore, friends, a slight illness has arisen in a bhikkhu. It occurs to him: ‘This slight illness has arisen in me. It is suitable to lie down. Let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy ... This is the seventh ground of laziness.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has recovered from an illness, not long after recovering from sickness. It occurs to him: ‘I have recovered from an illness, not long after recovering from sickness; my body is weak and unfit for work; let me lie down.’ So he lies down and does not arouse energy for the attainment of what has not yet been attained, for the achievement of what has not yet been achieved, and for the realization of what has not yet been realized. This is the eighth ground of laziness.

Eight grounds for arousing energy. Here, friends, a bhikkhu has work to do. It occurs to him: ‘I will have work to do, and while doing work it will not be easy to attend to the teaching of the Buddhas; let me arouse energy for the attainment of what has not yet been attained, for the achievement of what has not yet been achieved, and for the realization of what has not yet been realized.’ So he arouses energy for the attainment of what has not yet been attained, for the achievement of what has not yet been achieved, and for the realization of what has not yet been realized. This is the first ground for arousing energy.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has done some work. It occurs to him: ‘I have done work, and while I was doing work I was unable to attend to the teaching of the Buddhas; let me arouse energy ...’ So he arouses energy ... This is the second ground for arousing energy.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has a journey to travel. It occurs to him: ‘I will have a journey to travel, and while traveling the road it will not be easy to attend to the teaching of the Buddhas; let me arouse energy ...’ So he arouses energy ... This is the third ground for arousing energy.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has traveled a journey. It occurs to him: ‘I have traveled a journey, and while I was traveling the road I was unable to attend to the teaching of the Buddhas; let me arouse energy ...’ So he arouses energy ... This is the fourth ground for arousing energy.

Furthermore, friends, while wandering for alms in a village or town, a bhikkhu does not get his fill of coarse or fine food. It occurs to him: ‘While wandering for alms in a village or town, I did not get my fill of coarse or fine food; my body is light and fit for work; let me arouse energy ...’ So he arouses energy ... This is the fifth ground for arousing energy.

Furthermore, friends, while wandering for alms in a village or town, a bhikkhu gets his fill of coarse or fine food. It occurs to him: ‘While wandering for alms in a village or town, I got my fill of coarse or fine food; my body is strong and fit for work; let me arouse energy ...’ So he arouses energy ... This is the sixth ground for arousing energy.

Furthermore, friends, a slight illness has arisen in a bhikkhu. It occurs to him: ‘This slight illness has arisen in me, and it is possible that my illness may worsen; let me arouse energy ...’ So he arouses energy ... This is the seventh ground for arousing energy.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has recovered from an illness, not long after recovering from sickness. It occurs to him: ‘I have recovered from an illness, not long after recovering from sickness, and it is possible that my illness may relapse; let me arouse energy for the attainment of what has not yet been attained, for the achievement of what has not yet been achieved, and for the realization of what has not yet been realized.’ So he arouses energy for the attainment of what has not yet been attained, for the achievement of what has not yet been achieved, and for the realization of what has not yet been realized. Eight assemblies—the assembly of aristocrats, the assembly of brahmins, the assembly of householders, the assembly of ascetics, the assembly of the gods of the Four Great Kings, the assembly of the gods of the Thirty-Three, the assembly of Māras, and the assembly of Brahmās.

Eight worldly conditions—|gain::acquisition, profit [lābha]| and |loss::non-acquisition, deprivation [alābha]|, |fame::renown, status [yasa]| and |disgrace::disrepute, loss of status [ayasa]|, |blame::criticism, censure [nindā]| and |praise::commendation [pasaṁsā]|, and pleasure and pain.

#### 9. The Nines

There are, friends, nonads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One; herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What nonads?

Nine grounds for |resentment::malice, irritation, hostility [āghāta]|. One harbors resentment thinking: ‘They harmed me’; one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They are harming me’; one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They will harm me’; one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They harmed someone dear and agreeable to me’ ... one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They are harming someone dear and agreeable to me’ ... one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They will harm someone dear and agreeable to me’; one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They acted for the benefit of someone displeasing and disagreeable to me’ ... one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They are acting for the benefit of someone displeasing and disagreeable to me’ ... one harbors resentment thinking: ‘They will act for the benefit of someone displeasing and disagreeable to me.’

Nine ways of removing resentment. Thinking: ‘They harmed me, but what can be obtained here by harboring resentment?’ one removes resentment; thinking: ‘They are harming me, but what can be obtained here?’ one removes resentment; thinking: ‘They will harm me, but what can be obtained here?’ one removes resentment; thinking: ‘They harmed someone dear and agreeable to me ... they are harming ... they will harm someone dear and agreeable to me, but what can be obtained here?’ one removes resentment; thinking: ‘They acted for the benefit of someone displeasing and disagreeable to me ... they are acting ... they will act for the benefit of someone displeasing and disagreeable to me, but what can be obtained here?’ one removes resentment.

Nine abodes of sentient beings. There are, friends, sentient beings with diverse bodies and diverse perceptions, such as humans, certain gods, and some who have fallen into a realm of downfall. This is the first abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings with diverse bodies but identical perceptions, such as the gods of Brahmā’s assembly who are reborn through the first jhāna. This is the second abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings with identical bodies but diverse perceptions, such as the radiant gods. This is the third abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings with identical bodies and identical perceptions, such as the gods of Refulgent Glory. This is the fourth abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings who are |non-percipient::without conscious perception [asaññino]| and |non-experiencing::not feeling sensory impressions [appaṭisaṁvedino]|, such as the non-percipient gods. This is the fifth abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings who, with the complete surpassing of perceptions of form, with the disappearance of perceptions of sensory impact, and by not attending to perceptions of diversity, perceive ‘boundless space’ and reach the base of boundless space. This is the sixth abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings who, with the complete surpassing of the base of boundless space, perceive ‘boundless consciousness’ and reach the base of boundless consciousness. This is the seventh abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings who, with the complete surpassing of the base of boundless consciousness, perceive ‘there is nothing’ and reach the base of nothingness. This is the eighth abode of sentient beings.

There are, friends, sentient beings who, with the complete surpassing of the base of nothingness, reach the base of neither perception nor non-perception. This is the ninth abode of sentient beings.

Nine |inopportune moments::unfavorable conditions or times [akkhaṇā]| that are not occasions for living the spiritual life. Nine progressive abidings. With the settling of thought and examination ... he enters and dwells in the second jhāna. With the fading of desire for uplifting joy ... he enters and dwells in the third jhāna. With the abandoning of pleasure ... he enters and dwells in the fourth jhāna. With the complete surpassing of perceptions of form ... he enters and dwells in the base of boundless space. With the complete surpassing of the base of boundless space, aware that ‘consciousness is boundless,’ he enters and dwells in the base of boundless consciousness. With the complete surpassing of the base of boundless consciousness, aware that ‘there is nothing,’ he enters and dwells in the base of nothingness. With the complete surpassing of the base of nothingness, he enters and dwells in the base of neither perception nor non-perception. With the complete surpassing of the base of neither perception nor non-perception, he enters and dwells in the cessation of perception and what is felt.

Nine progressive cessations. For one who has attained the first jhāna, perceptions of sensuality have ceased. For one who has attained the second jhāna, thought and examination have ceased. For one who has attained the third jhāna, uplifting joy has ceased. For one who has attained the fourth jhāna, |in-breaths and out-breaths::respiration [assāsapassāsā]| have ceased. For one who has attained the base of boundless space, perceptions of form have ceased. For one who has attained the base of boundless consciousness, the perception of the base of boundless space has ceased. For one who has attained the base of nothingness, the perception of the base of boundless consciousness has ceased. For one who has attained the base of neither perception nor non-perception, the perception of the base of nothingness has ceased. For one who has attained the cessation of perception and what is felt, perception and what is felt have ceased.

These, friends, are the nonads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.

#### 10. The Tens

There are, friends, decads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert ... for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans. What decads?

Ten |qualities that provide a refuge::protector qualities [nāthakaraṇā dhammā]|. Here, friends, a bhikkhu is virtuous. He dwells restrained with the restraint of the |Pātimokkha::monastic code of discipline [pātimokkha]|, accomplished in conduct and resort, seeing danger in the slightest faults, and trains by undertaking the training precepts. That a bhikkhu, friends, is virtuous, dwells restrained with the restraint of the Pātimokkha, accomplished in conduct and resort, seeing danger in the slightest faults, and trains by undertaking the training precepts—this is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has learned much, remembers what he has learned, and consolidates what he has learned. Those teachings that are beneficial in the beginning, beneficial in the middle, and beneficial in the end, that are meaningful and well-expressed, and that proclaim the perfectly complete and purified spiritual life—such teachings as these he has learned extensively, remembered, recited verbally, mentally examined, and thoroughly penetrated by view. That a bhikkhu, friends, has learned much ... and thoroughly penetrated by view—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu has good friends, good companions, and good associates. That a bhikkhu, friends, has good friends, good companions, and good associates—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu is easy to admonish, endowed with qualities that make him easy to admonish, patient, and receives instruction respectfully. That a bhikkhu, friends, is easy to admonish ... and receives instruction respectfully—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, whatever diverse duties are to be done for his fellow spiritual companions, a bhikkhu is skillful and tireless in them, endowed with investigation into the means to carry them out and organize them properly. That a bhikkhu, friends, in whatever diverse duties are to be done for his fellow spiritual companions ... is able to organize them properly—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu loves the Dhamma, is pleasing in his assertions, and rejoices greatly in the higher Dhamma and higher Vinaya. That a bhikkhu, friends, loves the Dhamma ... and rejoices greatly in the higher Dhamma and higher Vinaya—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu is content with whatever robes, |alms food::::almsfood|, lodging, and medicines and requisites for the sick he gets. That a bhikkhu, friends, is content ... with whatever requisites he gets—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu dwells with energy aroused for the abandoning of unwholesome qualities and the acquisition of wholesome qualities; he is firm, steadfast in striving, and never lays down the yoke with regard to wholesome qualities. That a bhikkhu, friends, dwells with energy aroused ... and never lays down the yoke with regard to wholesome qualities—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu is mindful, endowed with supreme mindfulness and alertness, one who remembers and recollects what was done and said long ago. That a bhikkhu, friends, is mindful ... one who remembers and recollects—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Furthermore, friends, a bhikkhu is wise, endowed with wisdom that discerns arising and passing away, which is noble, penetrative, and leads to the complete wearing away of suffering. That a bhikkhu, friends, is wise ... leading to the complete wearing away of suffering—this too is a quality that provides a refuge.

Ten |kasiṇa bases::meditative totality fields used to develop deep unification of mind [kasiṇāyatanāni]|. One perceives the earth kasiṇa—above, below, across, undivided, and measureless. One perceives the water kasiṇa ... one perceives the fire kasiṇa ... one perceives the wind kasiṇa ... one perceives the blue kasiṇa ... one perceives the yellow kasiṇa ... one perceives the red kasiṇa ... one perceives the white kasiṇa ... one perceives the space kasiṇa ... one perceives the consciousness kasiṇa—above, below, across, undivided, and measureless.

Ten unwholesome courses of action—killing living beings, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech, divisive speech, harsh speech, frivolous chatter, |covetousness::longing for others' possessions [abhijjhā]|, ill will, and wrong view.

Ten wholesome courses of action—abstinence from killing living beings, abstinence from taking what is not given, abstinence from sexual misconduct, abstinence from false speech, abstinence from divisive speech, abstinence from harsh speech, abstinence from frivolous chatter, non-covetousness, non-ill will, and right view.

Ten noble abidings. Here, friends, a bhikkhu has abandoned five factors, is endowed with six factors, guards one factor, relies on four supports, has dispelled |sectarian truths::individual, dogmatic opinions [paccekasacca]|, has thoroughly given up searches, is |unclouded in intention::free from sensual, malicious, or harmful thoughts [anāvilasaṅkappa]|, has |tranquilized the bodily construct::stilled the in-breath and out-breath in the fourth jhāna [passaddhakāyasaṅkhāra]|, is well liberated in mind, and is well liberated by wisdom.

And how, friends, has a bhikkhu abandoned five factors? Here, friends, in a bhikkhu sensual desire is abandoned, ill will is abandoned, dullness and drowsiness are abandoned, restlessness and worry are abandoned, and doubt is abandoned. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu has abandoned five factors.

And how, friends, is a bhikkhu endowed with six factors? Here, friends, having seen a form with the eye, a bhikkhu is neither elated nor dejected, but abides equanimous, mindful, and clearly aware. Having heard a sound with the ear ... having cognized a mental object with the mind, he is neither elated nor dejected, but abides equanimous, mindful, and clearly aware. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu is endowed with six factors.

And how, friends, does a bhikkhu guard one factor? Here, friends, a bhikkhu is endowed with a mind guarded by mindfulness. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu guards one factor.

And how, friends, does a bhikkhu rely on four supports? Here, friends, after reflection, a bhikkhu uses some things, endures some things, avoids some things, and dispels some things. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu relies on four supports.

And how, friends, has a bhikkhu dispelled sectarian truths? Here, friends, whatever numerous sectarian truths are held by ordinary ascetics and brahmins, a bhikkhu has dispelled, driven out, given up, disgorged, released, abandoned, and relinquished them all. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu has dispelled sectarian truths.

And how, friends, has a bhikkhu thoroughly given up searches? Here, friends, in a bhikkhu the search for sensual pleasures is abandoned, the search for continued existence is abandoned, and the search for the spiritual life is tranquilized. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu has thoroughly given up searches.

And how, friends, is a bhikkhu unclouded in intention? Here, friends, in a bhikkhu the intention of sensuality is abandoned, the intention of ill will is abandoned, and the intention of harming is abandoned. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu is unclouded in intention.

And how, friends, has a bhikkhu tranquilized the bodily construct? Here, friends, with the abandoning of pleasure and pain, and with the prior settling down of mental pleasure and displeasure, a bhikkhu enters and dwells in the fourth jhāna, which is neither-painful-nor-pleasant and characterized by purification of mindfulness through equanimity. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu has tranquilized the bodily construct.

And how, friends, is a bhikkhu well liberated in mind? Here, friends, a bhikkhu’s mind is liberated from passion, liberated from aversion, and liberated from delusion. In this way, friends, a bhikkhu is well liberated in mind.

And how, friends, is a bhikkhu well liberated by wisdom? Here, friends, a bhikkhu understands: ‘Passion has been abandoned by me, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, obliterated, and rendered incapable of arising in the future.’ He understands: ‘Aversion has been abandoned by me, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, obliterated, and rendered incapable of arising in the future.’ He understands: ‘Delusion has been abandoned by me, cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, obliterated, and rendered incapable of arising in the future.’ In this way, friends, a bhikkhu is well liberated by wisdom.

Ten qualities of an adept—the right view of an adept, the right intention of an adept, the right speech of an adept, the right action of an adept, the right livelihood of an adept, the right effort of an adept, the right mindfulness of an adept, the right collectedness of an adept, the |right knowledge::direct knowledge of liberation [sammāñāṇa]| of an adept, and the |right liberation::unshakable freedom of an arahant [sammāvimutti]| of an adept.

These, friends, are the decads of principles rightly declared by the Blessed One who knows and sees, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One. Herein, all should recite in concert and not dispute, so that this spiritual life may last long and endure for a long time, for the welfare of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world, for the benefit, welfare, and happiness of gods and humans.”

Then the Blessed One rose and addressed the venerable Sāriputta: “Good, good, Sāriputta! Well indeed, Sāriputta, have you spoken the exposition for reciting in concert to the bhikkhus.”

The venerable Sāriputta said this, and the Teacher approved. Delighted, those bhikkhus rejoiced in what the venerable Sāriputta had said.

The Saṅgīti Sutta, the tenth, is finished.
