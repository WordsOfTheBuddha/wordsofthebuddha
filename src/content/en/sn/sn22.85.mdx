At one time, the venerable |Sāriputta::foremost disciple of the Buddha in great wisdom [sāriputta]| was dwelling at Sāvatthi, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

#### Yamaka’s Harmful View

Now at that time, a pernicious |view::misconception, speculative opinion [diṭṭhigata]| had arisen in a bhikkhu named Yamaka to this effect: “As I understand the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]| taught by the Blessed One, a bhikkhu |whose taints are exhausted::who has completely eradicated the mental defilements, an Arahant [khīṇāsava]|, with the breaking up of the body, is |annihilated::cut off, destroyed [ucchijjati]| and perishes, and does not exist after death.”

Several bhikkhus heard that such a pernicious view had arisen in the bhikkhu named Yamaka: “As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, a bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death.” Then those bhikkhus approached the venerable Yamaka; having approached, they exchanged friendly greetings with the venerable Yamaka.

After the exchange of a courteous and polite conversation, they sat down to one side. Seated to one side, those bhikkhus said this to the venerable Yamaka: “Is it true, friend Yamaka, that such a pernicious view has arisen in you: ‘As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, a bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death’?”

“Exactly so, friends, I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One: ‘A bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death.’”

“Do not say so, friend Yamaka, do not misrepresent the Blessed One. For it is not good to misrepresent the Blessed One. The Blessed One would not speak thus: ‘A bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death.’” Yet even when spoken to in this way by those bhikkhus, the venerable Yamaka still stubbornly held on to and adhered to that same pernicious view and declared: “As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, a bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death.”

Since those bhikkhus were unable to dissuade the venerable Yamaka from this pernicious view, they rose from their seats and approached the venerable Sāriputta. Having approached, they said this to the venerable Sāriputta: “Friend Sāriputta, such a pernicious view has arisen in the bhikkhu named Yamaka: ‘As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, a bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death.’ It would be good if the venerable Sāriputta would approach the bhikkhu Yamaka out of |compassion::benevolence, concern, gentle regard [anukampā]|.” The venerable Sāriputta consented in silence.

Then, in the late afternoon, having emerged from |seclusion::solitude, privacy [paṭisallāna]|, the venerable Sāriputta approached the venerable Yamaka. “Exactly so, friend, I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, that a bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death.”

#### Impermanence of the Aggregates

“What do you think, friend Yamaka, is |form::materiality, material existence, experience of the material world, i.e. encompassing both one’s body and external objects, whether near or far, gross or subtle, deficient or refined; first of the five aggregates [rūpa]| |permanent::stable, not in flux [nicca]| or |impermanent::not lasting, transient, unreliable [anicca]|?”

“Impermanent, friend.”

“Is |feeling::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, the experience felt on contact through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; second of the five aggregates [vedanā]| permanent <collapse>or impermanent</collapse> ... Is |perception::The mental process of recognizing and giving meaning to experience. It marks sensory information by signs, labels, or associations drawn from memory and the field of contact. Perception shapes how one experiences the world; third of the five aggregates [saññā]| ... Are |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]| ... Is |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object; fifth of the five aggregates [viññāṇa]| permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, friend.”

“Therefore, <collapse>friend Yamaka, any kind of form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, or consciousness whatsoever, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, deficient or refined, far or near—all should be seen with right wisdom as it truly is: ‘This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’</collapse> Seeing thus, <collapse>the learned disciple of the Noble Ones becomes disenchanted with form, becomes disenchanted with feeling, becomes disenchanted with perception, becomes disenchanted with intentional constructs, becomes disenchanted with consciousness. Experiencing disenchantment, he becomes dispassionate; through dispassion, he is liberated. When liberated, there arises the knowledge: ‘Liberated.’</collapse> He knows clearly: ‘<collapse>Birth is exhausted, the |spiritual life::a life of celibacy, contemplation, and ethical discipline lived for the sake of liberation; oriented toward inner development rather than sensual pleasures [brahmacariya]| has been lived, what had to be done has been done,</collapse> there is no more coming to any state of existence.’

#### Non-Apprehension of Tathāgata

What do you think, friend Yamaka, do you regard form as the |Tathāgata::one who has arrived at the truth, an epithet of a perfectly Awakened One [tathāgata]|?”

“No, friend.”

“Do you regard feeling as the Tathāgata?” “No, friend.” “Do you regard perception ... intentional constructs ... consciousness as the Tathāgata?”

“No, friend.”

“What do you think, friend Yamaka, do you regard the Tathāgata as in form?”

“No, friend.”

“Do you regard the Tathāgata as apart from form?”

“No, friend.”

“Do you regard the Tathāgata as in feeling? As apart from feeling? ... As in perception? As apart from perception? As in intentional constructs? As apart from intentional constructs? Do you regard the Tathāgata as in consciousness?”

“No, friend.”

“Do you regard the Tathāgata as apart from consciousness?”

“No, friend.”

“What do you think, friend Yamaka, do you regard form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness [taken together] as the Tathāgata?”

“No, friend.”

“What do you think, friend Yamaka, do you regard the Tathāgata as one who is without form, without feeling, without perception, without intentional constructs, without consciousness?”

“No, friend.”

“But here, friend Yamaka, when a Tathāgata is not to be apprehended by you as real and actual in this very life, is it fitting for you to declare: ‘As I understand the Dhamma taught by the Blessed One, a bhikkhu whose taints are exhausted, with the breaking up of the body, is annihilated and perishes, and does not exist after death’?”

“Friend Sāriputta, formerly, when I was |ignorant::unwise, unknowing [aviddasu]|, I had that pernicious view; but now, having heard this Dhamma teaching of the venerable Sāriputta, that pernicious view has been abandoned by me, and I have |made the breakthrough to::completely realized [abhisameta]| the Dhamma.”

#### An Arahant After Death

“If, friend Yamaka, they were to ask you thus: ‘Friend Yamaka, as to that bhikkhu who is an |Arahant::a worthy one, a fully awakened being [arahant]|, one whose taints are exhausted—with the breaking up of the body, after death, what becomes of him?’ Being asked thus, friend Yamaka, how would you answer?”

“If, friend, they were to ask me thus: ‘Friend Yamaka, as to that bhikkhu who is an Arahant, one whose taints are exhausted—with the breaking up of the body, after death, what becomes of him?’ Being asked thus, friend, I would answer in this way: ‘Friends, form is impermanent. What is impermanent is |unsatisfactory::distressful, unfulfilling, marked by discontent, suffering [dukkha]|; what is unsatisfactory has |ceased::ended [niruddha]| and |passed away::disappeared, subsided [atthaṅgata]|.

Feeling ... Perception ... Intentional constructs ... Consciousness is impermanent. What is impermanent is unsatisfactory; what is unsatisfactory has ceased and passed away.’ Being asked thus, friend, I would answer in this way.”

#### Simile of the Treacherous Servant

“Good, good, friend Yamaka! Well then, friend Yamaka, I shall give you a simile for an even greater |insight::knowing, understanding [ñāṇa]| into this very meaning. Suppose, friend Yamaka, there were a householder or a householder’s son—rich, affluent, and wealthy—and he was well-protected. Then a certain man might appear desiring his ruin, desiring his harm, desiring his insecurity, wishing to deprive him of life. It would occur to him: ‘This householder or householder’s son is rich, affluent, and wealthy, and he is well-protected; it is not easy to deprive him of life by force. Suppose I were to |ingratiate myself::insinuate myself, get close to [anupakhajja]| and then deprive him of life.’ So he would approach that householder or householder’s son and say thus: ‘Sir, I would serve you.’ Then that householder or householder’s son would employ him as a servant. He would serve him, rising before him, sleeping after him, willing to do whatever he commands, agreeable in conduct, and speaking pleasantly. That householder or householder’s son would regard him as a friend, would regard him as a companion, and would place trust in him. When, friend, it occurred to that man: ‘This householder or householder’s son has complete trust in me,’ then, knowing him to be alone, he would deprive him of life with a sharp blade.

What do you think, friend Yamaka? When that man approached that householder or householder’s son and said thus: ‘Sir, I would serve you,’ even then he was a |murderer::killer, executioner, deadly foe [vadhaka]|, yet though he was a murderer, the householder did not know: ‘He is my murderer.’ And when he served him—rising before him, sleeping after him, willing to do whatever he commands, agreeable in conduct, and speaking pleasantly—even then he was a murderer, yet though he was a murderer, the householder did not know: ‘He is my murderer.’ And when, knowing him to be alone, he deprived him of life with a sharp blade, even then he was a murderer, yet though he was a murderer, the householder did not know: ‘He is my murderer’?”

“Yes, friend.”
