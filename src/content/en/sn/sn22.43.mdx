description: The Buddha urges the bhikkhus to dwell with themselves and the Dhamma as island and refuge, and to examine the origin of sorrow in regarding the five aggregates as self—showing that seeing their impermanence abandons that sorrow and brings ease.

At Sāvatthi.

“Dwell |with yourselves as your own island::independent, self-reliant; lit. own island [attadīpa]|, |bhikkhus,::::| with yourselves as your own refuge, with no other refuge; dwell with the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]| as your island, with the Dhamma as your refuge, with no other refuge.

For those who dwell with themselves as their own island, |bhikkhus,::::| with themselves as their own refuge, with no other refuge; with the Dhamma as their island, with the Dhamma as their refuge, with no other refuge, the |origin::cause; lit. womb [yoni]| should be carefully examined: ‘What is the origin of sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair; from what do they arise?’

And what|, bhikkhus,::::| is the origin of sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair; from what do they arise? Here, |bhikkhus,::::| an |uninstructed::uninitiated, untaught, untrained [assutavant]| ordinary person, |who has no regard for::lit. who has not seen [adassāvī]| the Noble Ones and is unskilled and untrained in the Dhamma of the Noble Ones; who has no regard for persons of integrity and is unskilled and untrained in the Dhamma of persons of integrity—perceives |form::materiality, material existence, experience of the material world, i.e. encompassing both one’s body and external objects, whether near or far, gross or subtle, deficient or refined; first of the five aggregates [rūpa]| as self, or self as possessing form, or form as being in self, or self as being in form. That form of his then changes and becomes otherwise. With the change and becoming otherwise of form, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair.

He perceives |feeling::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, the experience felt on contact through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; second of the five aggregates [vedanā]| as self, or self as possessing feeling, or feeling as being in self, or self as being in feeling. That feeling of his then changes and becomes otherwise. With the change and becoming otherwise of feeling, there arise sorrow, lamentation<collapse>, pain, displeasure, and</collapse> despair.

He perceives |perception::The mental process of recognizing and giving meaning to experience. It marks sensory information by signs, labels, or associations drawn from memory and the field of contact. Perception shapes how one experiences the world; third of the five aggregates [saññā]| as self<collapse>, or self as possessing perception, or perception as being in self, or self as being in perception. That perception of his then changes and becomes otherwise. With the change and becoming otherwise of perception, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair.</collapse>

He perceives |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]| as self<collapse>, or self as possessing intentional constructs, or intentional constructs as being in self, or self as being in intentional constructs. Those intentional constructs of his then change and become otherwise. With the change and becoming otherwise of intentional constructs, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair.</collapse>

He perceives |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object [viññāṇa]| as self, or self as possessing consciousness, or consciousness as being in self, or self as being in consciousness. That consciousness of his then changes and becomes otherwise. With the change and becoming otherwise of consciousness, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair.

But of form itself, |bhikkhus,::::| having known impermanence, change, |fading of desire::dispassion, detachment [virāga]|, and |cessation::ending, termination [nirodha]|—[seeing] ‘Both formerly and now, all form is |impermanent::not lasting, transient, unreliable [anicca]|, |unsatisfactory::uncomfortable, unpleasant [dukkha]|, and |subject to change::of the nature of alteration, decay [vipariṇāmadhamma]|’—thus seeing this |as it truly is::as it has come to be, as it is in actuality [yathābhūta]| with |correct wisdom::right distinctive knowledge [sammappaññā]|, whatever sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair there are, those are abandoned. With their abandonment, one is not |perturbed::agitated, shaken up [paritassati]|; being unperturbed, one dwells at ease. A bhikkhu who dwells at ease is called ‘|quenched in that respect::liberated from mental defilements as to that factor or aspect [tadaṅganibbuta]|.’

But of feeling itself, |bhikkhus,::::| having known impermanence, change, fading of desire, and cessation—[seeing] ‘Both formerly and now, all feeling is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change’—thus seeing this as it truly is with correct wisdom, whatever sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair there are, those are abandoned. With their abandonment, one is not perturbed; being unperturbed, one dwells at ease. A bhikkhu who dwells at ease is called ‘quenched in that respect.’

But of perception itself, |bhikkhus,::::|<collapse> having known impermanence, change, fading of desire, and cessation—[seeing] ‘Both formerly and now, all perception is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change’—thus seeing this as it truly is with correct wisdom, whatever sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair there are, those are abandoned. With their abandonment, one is not perturbed; being unperturbed, one dwells at ease. A bhikkhu who dwells at ease is called ‘quenched in that respect.’</collapse>

But of intentional constructs themselves, |bhikkhus,::::| having known impermanence, change, fading of desire, and cessation—[seeing] ‘Both formerly and now, all intentional constructs are impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change’—thus seeing this as it truly is with correct wisdom, whatever sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair there are, those are abandoned. With their abandonment, one is not perturbed; being unperturbed, one dwells at ease. A bhikkhu who dwells at ease is called ‘quenched in that respect.’

But of consciousness itself, |bhikkhus,::::| having known impermanence, change, fading of desire, and cessation—[seeing] ‘Both formerly and now, all consciousness is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change’—thus seeing this as it truly is with correct wisdom, whatever sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair there are, those are abandoned. With their abandonment, one is not perturbed; being unperturbed, one dwells at ease. A bhikkhu who dwells at ease is called ‘quenched in that respect.’”
