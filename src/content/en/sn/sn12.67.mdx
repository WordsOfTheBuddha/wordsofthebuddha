At one time, the venerable |Sāriputta::foremost disciple of the Buddha in great wisdom [sāriputta]| and the venerable |Mahākoṭṭhika::foremost disciple of the Buddha in analytical understanding [Mahākoṭṭhika]| were staying in the Deer Park at Isipatana near Varanasi.

Then, when it was late afternoon, the venerable Mahākoṭṭhika emerged from |seclusion::solitude, privacy [paṭisallāna]| and approached the venerable Sāriputta. Having drawn near, he exchanged friendly greetings with the venerable Sāriputta. After the exchange of a courteous and polite conversation, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the venerable Mahākoṭṭhika said to the venerable Sāriputta:

“How is it, friend Sāriputta: is aging and death self-created, created by another, both self-created and created by another, or is aging and death neither self-created nor created by another, but |arisen by chance::spontaneously arisen [adhiccasamuppanna]|?”

“Aging and death, friend Koṭṭhika, is not self-created, nor is it created by another, nor is it both self-created and created by another, nor is it neither self-created nor created by another, arisen by chance. Rather, with dependence on |birth::rebirth, conception, coming into existence [jāti]|, there is aging and death.”

“How is it, friend Sāriputta: is birth self-created, created by another, both self-created and created by another, or is birth neither self-created nor created by another, but arisen by chance?”

“Birth, friend Koṭṭhika, is not self-created, nor is it created by another, nor is it both self-created and created by another, nor is it neither self-created nor created by another, arisen by chance. Rather, with dependence on |existence::being, becoming; continued conditional existence, the karmically conditioned mode of being that leads to future rebirth [bhava]|, there is birth.”

“How is it, friend Sāriputta: is existence self-created ... is |clinging::grasping, acquiring, appropriating, taking possession, identifying; fuel for fire; lit. taking near [upādāna]| self-created ... is |craving::wanting, yearning, longing, attachment, lit. thirst [taṇha]| self-created ... is |felt experience::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, feeling, second of the five aggregates [vedanā]| self-created ... is |contact::sense impingement, raw experience, touch [phassa]| self-created ... are the |six sense bases::The six internal sense bases—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind—are the faculties that enable sensory contact and experience. They are not the physical organs themselves, but the functional conditions that enable consciousness to meet an object [saḷāyatana]| self-created ... are |name and form::mentality and materiality—the integrated structure of mental capacities (intention, attention, contact, feeling, perception) and physical form that together constitute and sustain an individual being [nāmarūpa]| self-created, created by another, both self-created and created by another, or are name and form neither self-created nor created by another, but arisen by chance?”

“Name and form, friend Koṭṭhika, are not self-created, nor are they created by another, nor are they both self-created and created by another, nor are they neither self-created nor created by another, arisen by chance. Rather, with dependence on |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object [viññāṇa]|, there are name and form.”

“How is it, friend Sāriputta: is consciousness self-created, created by another, both self-created and created by another, or is consciousness neither self-created nor created by another, but arisen by chance?”

“Consciousness, friend Koṭṭhika, is not self-created, nor is it created by another, nor is it both self-created and created by another, nor is it neither self-created nor created by another, arisen by chance. Rather, with dependence on name and form, there is consciousness.”

“Just now we understood the venerable Sāriputta’s statement as follows: ‘Name and form, friend Koṭṭhika, are not self-created, nor are they created by another, nor are they both self-created and created by another, nor are they neither self-created nor created by another, arisen by chance. Rather, with dependence on consciousness, there are name and form.’

And just now we also understood the venerable Sāriputta’s statement as follows: ‘Consciousness, friend Koṭṭhika, is not self-created, nor is it created by another, nor is it both self-created and created by another, nor is it neither self-created nor created by another, arisen by chance. Rather, with dependence on name and form, there is consciousness.’

How, friend Sāriputta, should the meaning of this statement be regarded?”

“In that case, friend, I shall give you a simile, for there are some wise persons who understand the meaning of a statement through a simile. Suppose, friend, two sheaves of reeds were to stand leaning against each other.

In the same way, friend, with dependence on name and form, there is consciousness; with dependence on consciousness, there are name and form; with dependence on name and form, there are the six sense bases; with dependence on the six sense bases, there is contact; <collapse>with dependence on contact, there is felt experience; with dependence on felt experience, there is craving; with dependence on craving, there is clinging; with dependence on clinging, there is existence; with dependence on existence, there is birth; with dependence on birth, aging and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair arise.</collapse> Thus is the arising of this entire heap of suffering. If, friend, one of those sheaves of reeds were to be pulled away, the other would fall down; and if the other were to be pulled away, the first would fall down.

In the same way, friend, with the cessation of name and form, consciousness ceases; with the cessation of consciousness, name and form cease; with the cessation of name and form, the six sense bases cease; with the cessation of the six sense bases, contact ceases; <collapse>with the cessation of contact, felt experience ceases; with the cessation of felt experience, craving ceases; with the cessation of craving, clinging ceases; with the cessation of clinging, existence ceases; with the cessation of existence, birth ceases; with the cessation of birth, aging and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair cease.</collapse> Thus is the cessation of this entire heap of suffering.”

“It is wonderful, friend Sāriputta! It is marvelous, friend Sāriputta! How well spoken this is by the venerable Sāriputta! And we rejoice in what has been spoken by the venerable Sāriputta on these thirty-six grounds:

‘If, friend, a bhikkhu teaches the Dhamma for |disenchantment::de-illusionment, disinterest, dispassion [nibbidā]|, for the |fading of desire::dispassion, detachment [virāga]|, and for the |cessation::ending, termination [nirodha]| of aging and death, he is fit to be called “a bhikkhu who speaks on the Dhamma.” If, friend, a bhikkhu practices for disenchantment, for the fading of desire, and for the cessation of aging and death, he is fit to be called “a bhikkhu who practices in accordance with the Dhamma.” If, friend, a bhikkhu is liberated |without clinging::without grasping [anupādā]| through disenchantment, through the fading of desire, and through the cessation of aging and death, he is fit to be called “a bhikkhu who has attained Nibbāna in this very life.” If, friend, a bhikkhu teaches the Dhamma for disenchantment regarding birth ... regarding existence ... regarding clinging ... regarding craving ... regarding felt experience ... regarding contact ... regarding the six sense bases ... regarding name and form ... regarding consciousness ... regarding |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]| ... regarding |ignorance::fundamental unawareness or misunderstanding of the true nature of reality, not experientially understanding the four noble truths [avijjā]|, for its fading of desire, and for its cessation, he is fit to be called “a bhikkhu who speaks on the Dhamma.” If, friend, a bhikkhu practices for disenchantment, for the fading of desire, and for the cessation of ignorance, he is fit to be called “a bhikkhu who practices in accordance with the Dhamma.” If, friend, a bhikkhu is liberated without clinging through disenchantment, through the fading of desire, and through the cessation of ignorance, he is fit to be called “a bhikkhu who has attained Nibbāna in this very life.”’”
