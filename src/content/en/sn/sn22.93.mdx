At Sāvatthi.

“Suppose, |bhikkhus,::::| there is a mountain river flowing far, with a swift current, carrying things away. On both its banks, if |kāsa grass::a coarse grass that grows on riverbanks; Saccharum spontaneum [kāsa]| had grown, it would hang down over the river; if |kusa grass::sharp-bladed grass used in ancient India in ceremonies and symbolic contexts [kusa]| had grown, it would hang down over the river; if |reeds::rush, sedge growing along riverbanks [pabbaja]| had grown, they would hang down over the river; if |vetiver grass::a fragrant grass known for its deep, tangled root system. It spreads aggressively when watered. [bīraṇa]| had grown, it would hang down over the river; if trees had grown, they would hang down over the river. A man being swept away by its current, if he were to grab the kāsa grass, it would |break off::break apart, disintegrate [palujjati]|. He would thereby meet with |misfortune and disaster::tragedy and ruin [anayabyasana]|. If he were to grab the kusa grass, or the reeds, or the vetiver grass, or the trees, they would break off. He would thereby meet with misfortune and disaster.

Even so, |bhikkhus,::::| an |uninstructed::uninitiated, untaught, untrained [assutavant]| ordinary person, |who has no regard for::lit. who has not seen [adassāvī]| the Noble Ones and is unskilled and untrained in the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]| of the Noble Ones; who has no regard for persons of integrity and is unskilled and untrained in the Dhamma of persons of integrity—

perceives |form::materiality, material existence, experience of the material world, i.e. encompassing both one’s body and external objects, whether near or far, gross or subtle, deficient or refined; first of the five aggregates [rūpa]| as self, or self as possessing form, or form as being in self, or self as being in form. That form of his then breaks apart. He thereby meets with misfortune and disaster. He perceives |feeling::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, the experience felt on contact through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; second of the five aggregates [vedanā]| <collapse>as self, or self as possessing feeling, or feeling as being in self, or self as being in feeling. That feeling of his then breaks apart. He thereby meets with misfortune and disaster. He perceives</collapse> |perception::The mental process of recognizing and giving meaning to experience. It marks sensory information by signs, labels, or associations drawn from memory and the field of contact. Perception shapes how one experiences the world; third of the five aggregates [saññā]| <collapse>as self, or self as possessing perception, or perception as being in self, or self as being in perception. That perception of his then breaks apart. He thereby meets with misfortune and disaster. He perceives</collapse> |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]| <collapse>as self, or self as possessing intentional constructs, or intentional constructs as being in self, or self as being in intentional constructs. Those intentional constructs of his then break apart. He thereby meets with misfortune and disaster. He perceives</collapse> |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object [viññāṇa]| as self, or self as possessing consciousness, or consciousness as being in self, or self as being in consciousness. That consciousness of his then breaks apart. He thereby meets with misfortune and disaster.

What do you think, |bhikkhus,::::| is form |permanent::stable, not in flux [nicca]| or |impermanent::not lasting, transient, unreliable [anicca]|?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.” <collapse>“And that which is impermanent—is it unsatisfactory or pleasant?” “Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.” “And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self’?” “Indeed not, venerable sir.”</collapse>

“Are feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.”

“Therefore, <collapse>bhikkhus, any kind of form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, consciousness, whatsoever, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, deficient or refined, far or near—all form is to be seen with proper wisdom as it truly is: ‘This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’ Seeing thus, bhikkhus, the learned disciple of the Noble Ones becomes disenchanted with form, becomes disenchanted with feeling, becomes disenchanted with perception, becomes disenchanted with intentional constructs, becomes disenchanted with consciousness. Experiencing disenchantment, they become dispassionate; through dispassion, there is release. When released, there arises the knowledge: ‘Released.’ He</collapse> knows clearly: ‘Birth is exhausted, the |spiritual life::a life of celibacy, contemplation, and ethical discipline lived for the sake of liberation; oriented toward inner development rather than sensual pleasures [brahmacariya]| has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of existence.’”
