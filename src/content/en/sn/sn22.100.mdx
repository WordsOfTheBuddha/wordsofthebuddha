---
title: Dutiya gaddulabaddha sutta - Tied with a Leash (Second)
description: Cyclic existence is without a conceivable beginning. Like a dog tied to a post, the uninstructed person stays bound to the five aggregates. The mind is more diverse than any picture or than the animal realm; like a painter, one produces only the aggregates. Seeing them as they truly are, there is no more coming to any state of existence.
qualities: recognition of impermanence, passion, aversion, delusion, ignorance, craving, self-making
theme: wisdom, inspiration, urgency

At Sāvatthi.

“|Bhikkhus,::::| this |cyclic existence::wandering from one state of existence to another, the cycle of birth and death, moving on continuously [saṁsāra]| has an inconceivable beginning. A first point is not evident of beings who, obstructed by |ignorance::fundamental unawareness or misunderstanding of the true nature of reality, not experientially understanding the four noble truths [avijjā]| and fettered by |craving::wanting, yearning, longing, attachment, lit. thirst [taṇha]|, run on and wander in this cycle of existence. Suppose|, bhikkhus,::::| a dog tied with a leash was bound to a strong post or pillar. If it goes, it goes up to that same post or pillar; if it stands, it stands close to that same post or pillar; if it sits, it sits close to that same post or pillar; if it lies down, it lies down close to that same post or pillar.

Even so, |bhikkhus,::::| an |uninstructed::uninitiated, untaught, untrained [assutavant]| ordinary person perceives |form::materiality, material existence, experience of the material world, i.e. encompassing both one’s body and external objects, whether near or far, gross or subtle, deficient or refined; first of the five aggregates [rūpa]| as ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self.’ They perceive |feeling::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, the experience felt on contact through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; second of the five aggregates [vedanā]| as ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self.’ <collapse>They perceive |perception::The mental process of recognizing and giving meaning to experience. It marks sensory information by signs, labels, or associations drawn from memory and the field of contact. Perception shapes how one experiences the world; third of the five aggregates [saññā]| as ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self.’ They perceive |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]| as ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self.’</collapse> They perceive |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object; fifth of the five aggregates [viññāṇa]| as ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self.’ If they go, they go up to these |five aggregates subject to clinging::the physical and mental heaps that are appropriated, grasped at, or taken as self; the fivefold collection of form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness bound up with attachment [pañca + upādānakkhandha]|; if they stand, they stand close to these five aggregates subject to clinging; if they sit, they sit close to these five aggregates subject to clinging; if they lie down, they lie down close to these five aggregates subject to clinging.

Therefore, |bhikkhus,::::| one’s own mind should be |frequently reviewed::regularly considered, contemplated, reflected upon [abhikkhaṇa + paccavekkhitabba]|: ‘For a long time this mind has been |defiled::tainted, tarnished [saṅkiliṭṭha]| by |passion::intense desire, strong emotion, infatuation, obsession, lust [rāga]|, |aversion::hatred, hostility, mental attitude of rejection, fault-finding, resentful disapproval [dosa]|, and |delusion::illusion, misperception, erroneous belief, false idea, misapprehension; a fundamental distortion of reality that sustains confusion, clouds discernment, and fuels further doubt [moha]|.’ By the |defilement of the mind::corruption, impurity of mind [cittasaṅkilesa]| are beings defiled; by the |purification of the mind::cleansing of mind [cittavodāna]| are beings purified.

Have you seen|, bhikkhus,::::| the picture called ‘|Caraṇa::animation, moving picture; (comm) artwork which was carried around [caraṇa]::Charaṇa|’?”

“Yes, venerable sir.”

“Even that picture called ‘|Caraṇa::::Charaṇa|’ has been |crafted::fashioned, thought up [cittita]| by the mind. Yet the mind is even |more picturesque::more fancy, more painted [cittatara]| than that picture called ‘|Caraṇa::::Charaṇa|.’

Therefore, |bhikkhus,::::| one’s own mind should be frequently reviewed: ‘For a long time this mind has been defiled by passion, aversion, and delusion.’ By the defilement of the mind are beings defiled; by the purification of the mind are beings purified.

I do not see even a single other class of beings|, bhikkhus,::::| as picturesque as beings in the animal realm. Even those beings in the animal realm are also crafted by the mind. Yet the mind is even more picturesque than those beings in the animal realm.

Therefore, |bhikkhus,::::| one’s own mind should be frequently reviewed: ‘For a long time this mind has been defiled by passion, aversion, and delusion.’ By the defilement of the mind are beings defiled; by the purification of the mind are beings purified.

Suppose|, bhikkhus,::::| an artist or a |painter::picture-maker; lit. mind-maker, playing on citta as both ‘mind’ and ‘picture’ [cittakāra]|, using dye or lac or turmeric or indigo or crimson, would create a feminine form or a masculine form, complete in all its parts, on a well-polished plank, or a wall, or a canvas.

Even so, |bhikkhus,::::| an uninstructed ordinary person, producing form, |produces::brings into being, generates, gives rise to [abhinibbatteti]| only form; <collapse>producing feeling, produces only feeling; producing perception, produces only perception; producing intentional constructs, produces only intentional constructs;</collapse> producing consciousness, produces only consciousness.

What do you think, |bhikkhus,::::| is form |permanent::stable, not in flux [nicca]| or |impermanent::not lasting, transient, unreliable [anicca]|?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.”

“Is feeling ... perception ... intentional constructs ... consciousness permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.” <collapse>“And that which is impermanent—is it |unsatisfactory::uncomfortable, unpleasant [dukkha]| or |pleasant::comfortable, easy, good [sukha]|?” “Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.” “And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and |subject to change::of the nature of alteration, decay [vipariṇāmadhamma]|—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am,</collapse> this is my self’?” “Indeed not, venerable sir.” “Therefore, <collapse>any kind of form whatsoever, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, deficient or refined, far or near—all form is to be seen with proper wisdom as it truly is: ‘This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’ Any kind of feeling whatsoever, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, deficient or refined, far or near—all feeling is to be seen with proper wisdom as it truly is: ‘This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’ Any kind of perception whatsoever, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, deficient or refined, far or near—all perception is to be seen with proper wisdom as it truly is: ‘This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’ Any kind of intentional constructs whatsoever, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, deficient or refined, far or near—all intentional constructs are to be seen with proper wisdom as they truly are: ‘These are not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’ Any kind of consciousness whatsoever, whether past, future, or present, internal or external, gross or subtle, deficient or refined, far or near—all consciousness is to be seen with proper wisdom as it truly is: ‘This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.’ Seeing thus, the learned disciple of the Noble Ones becomes disenchanted with form, becomes disenchanted with feeling, becomes disenchanted with perception, becomes disenchanted with intentional constructs, becomes disenchanted with consciousness. Experiencing disenchantment, they become dispassionate; through dispassion, there is release. When released, there arises the knowledge: ‘Released.’ They</collapse> knows clearly: <collapse>‘Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done,</collapse> there is no more coming to any state of existence.’”
