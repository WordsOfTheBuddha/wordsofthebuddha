Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling among the Sakyans at a Sakyan market town named Devadaha.

Then several bhikkhus bound for the western lands went to the Blessed One, and after paying homage to him, they sat down to one side. Sitting to one side, those bhikkhus said to the Blessed One: “Venerable sir, we wish to go to the western lands and take up residence in the western lands.”

“Have you taken leave of |Sāriputta::foremost disciple of the Buddha in great wisdom [sāriputta]|, bhikkhus?”

“No, venerable sir, we have not taken leave of the venerable Sāriputta.”

“Take leave of Sāriputta, bhikkhus. Sāriputta, bhikkhus, is |wise::learned, astute, discerning [paṇḍita]| and helpful to the bhikkhus, his companions in the spiritual life.”

“Yes, venerable sir,” those bhikkhus replied to the Blessed One.

Now on that occasion, the venerable Sāriputta was seated not far from the Blessed One in a certain clump of golden shower trees. Then those bhikkhus, having delighted and rejoiced in the Blessed One’s words, rose from their seats, and after paying homage to the Blessed One, keeping him on their right, they approached the venerable Sāriputta. Having approached, they exchanged friendly greetings with the venerable Sāriputta.

After the exchange of a courteous and polite conversation, they sat down to one side. Seated to one side, those bhikkhus said this to the venerable Sāriputta: “Friend Sāriputta, we wish to go to the western lands and take up residence in the western lands. We have taken leave of the Teacher.”

“Friends, when a bhikkhu goes to foreign lands, there are those who will question him—learned nobles, learned brahmins, learned householders, and learned ascetics. For wise people, friends, are inquisitive: ‘What does your Teacher teach? What does he proclaim?’ I hope, venerable ones, that you have well heard, well grasped, well attended to, well sustained, and well penetrated the teachings with wisdom, so that when answering you will state what has been said by the Blessed One and not misrepresent the Blessed One with what is contrary to fact; that you will explain in accordance with the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]|, and that no reasonable consequence of your statement would afford grounds for criticism.”

“Even from far away, friend, we would come to venerable Sāriputta to learn the meaning of this statement. It would be good if venerable Sāriputta himself would clarify the meaning of this statement.”

“Then, friends, listen and pay close attention; I will speak.”

“Yes, friend,” those bhikkhus replied to the venerable Sāriputta. The venerable Sāriputta said this:

“Friends, when a bhikkhu goes to foreign lands, there are those who will question him—learned nobles, learned brahmins, learned householders, and learned ascetics. For wise people, friends, are inquisitive: ‘What does your Teacher teach? What does he proclaim?’ When questioned thus, friends, you should answer: ‘Friends, our Teacher teaches the removal of |desire and attachment::desire-passion, sensual craving, intention of sensuality and lust [chandarāga]|.’

Even when you answer thus, friends, there may still be those who question you further—learned nobles, learned brahmins, learned householders, and learned ascetics. For wise people, friends, are inquisitive: ‘Regarding what does your Teacher teach the removal of desire and attachment?’ When questioned thus, friends, you should answer: ‘Friends, our Teacher teaches the removal of desire and attachment toward |form::materiality, material existence, experience of the material world, i.e. encompassing both one’s body and external objects, whether near or far, gross or subtle, deficient or refined; first of the five aggregates [rūpa]|, toward |feeling::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, the experience felt on contact through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; second of the five aggregates [vedanā]|, toward |perception::The mental process of recognizing and giving meaning to experience. It marks sensory information by signs, labels, or associations drawn from memory and the field of contact. Perception shapes how one experiences the world; third of the five aggregates [saññā]|, toward |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]|, and toward |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object [viññāṇa]|.’

Even when you answer thus, friends, there may still be those who question you further—learned nobles, learned brahmins, learned householders, and learned ascetics. For wise people, friends, are inquisitive: ‘Seeing what |drawback::disadvantage, unsatisfactoriness, inadequacy, danger [ādīnava]| does your Teacher teach the removal of desire and attachment toward form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness?’ When questioned thus, friends, you should answer: ‘Friends, for one who is not free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, |fever::mental torment, distress, strong desire, discomfort [pariḷāha]|, and craving for form, with the change and alteration of that form, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For feeling<collapse>, for one who is not free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of that feeling, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For</collapse> perception<collapse>, for one who is not free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of that perception, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For</collapse> intentional constructs<collapse>, for one who is not free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of those intentional constructs, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For</collapse> consciousness, for one who is not free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of that consciousness, there arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. It is seeing this drawback, friends, that our Teacher teaches the removal of desire and attachment toward form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness.’

Even when you answer thus, friends, there may still be those who question you further—learned nobles, learned brahmins, learned householders, and learned ascetics. For wise people, friends, are inquisitive: ‘Seeing what |benefit::advantage, blessing, reward [ānisaṁsa]| does your Teacher teach the removal of desire and attachment toward form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness?’ When questioned thus, friends, you should answer: ‘Friends, for one who is free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving for form, with the change and alteration of that form, there do not arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For feeling<collapse>, for one who is free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of that feeling, there do not arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For</collapse> perception<collapse>, for one who is free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of that perception, there do not arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For</collapse> intentional constructs<collapse>, for one who is free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of those intentional constructs, there do not arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. For</collapse> consciousness, for one who is free from passion, desire, affection, thirst, fever, and craving, with the change and alteration of that consciousness, there do not arise sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair. It is seeing this benefit, friends, that our Teacher teaches the removal of desire and attachment toward form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness.’

Friends, if one who enters upon and dwells in |unwholesome::unhealthy, unbeneficial, harmful [akusala]| qualities were to dwell happily in this very life—without distress, without anguish, without fever—and after the breaking up of the body, after death, a |good destination::fortunate condition [sugati]| were to be expected, the Blessed One would not praise the abandoning of unwholesome qualities. But because, friends, one who enters upon and dwells in unwholesome qualities dwells in suffering in this very life—with distress, with anguish, with fever—and after the breaking up of the body, after death, a |bad destination::state of misery [duggati]| is to be expected, therefore the Blessed One praises the abandoning of unwholesome qualities.

And friends, if one who enters upon and dwells in |wholesome::healthy, beneficial, useful [kusala]| qualities were to dwell in suffering in this very life—with distress, with anguish, with fever—and after the breaking up of the body, after death, a bad destination were to be expected, the Blessed One would not praise the acquisition of wholesome qualities. But because, friends, one who enters upon and dwells in wholesome qualities dwells happily in this very life—without distress, without anguish, without fever—and after the breaking up of the body, after death, a good destination is to be expected, therefore the Blessed One praises the acquisition of wholesome qualities.”

The venerable Sāriputta said this. The bhikkhus were delighted and pleased with venerable Sāriputta’s words.
