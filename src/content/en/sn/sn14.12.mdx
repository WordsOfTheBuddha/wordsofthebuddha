---
title: Sanidāna sutta - With A Cause
description: The Buddha explains how thoughts of sensuality, ill will, and harming arise from a cause and how to abandon them.
qualities: giving up, sensual desire, aversion, cruelty, loving-kindness, non-harm
theme: cultivating discernment, wisdom
slug: sn14.12
simile: burning grass torch thrown into a thicket of dry grass
---

At Sāvatthi.

“A |thought of sensuality::thought related to enjoyment of sensual pleasures, sexual thought [kāmavitakka]| arises from a cause, |bhikkhus,::::| not without a cause; a |thought of ill will::intentional thought of mental rejection, hostility, or malevolence toward others; deliberate contemplation rooted in antipathy or wishing harm [byāpādavitakka]| arises from a cause, not without a cause; a |thought of harming::thought of cruelty, violence, or aggression [vihiṃsāvitakka]| arises from a cause, not without a cause.

And how|, bhikkhus,::::| does a thought of sensuality arise from a cause, not without a cause? How does a thought of ill will arise from a cause, not without a cause? How does a thought of harming arise from a cause, not without a cause? Dependent on the |element of sensuality::element of sensual desire [kāmadhātu]|, |bhikkhus,::::| there arises the |perception::The mental process of recognizing and giving meaning to experience. It marks sensory information by signs, labels, or associations drawn from memory and the field of contact. Perception shapes how one experiences the world; third of the five aggregates [saññā]| of sensuality; dependent on the perception of sensuality arises the |intention::thought, volition, conception, plan [saṅkappa]| of sensuality; dependent on the intention of sensuality arises sensual desire; dependent on sensual desire arises sensual |fever::mental torment, distress, strong desire, discomfort [pariḷāha]|; dependent on sensual fever arises the |search for sensual pleasures::looking for objects of sensual desire [kāmapariyesanā]|. When engaged in a search for sensual pleasures, |bhikkhus,::::| the uninstructed ordinary person practices improperly in three ways: by way of body, speech, and mind.

Dependent on the element of ill will, |bhikkhus,::::| there arises the perception of ill will; dependent on the perception of ill will arises the intention of ill will; dependent on the intention of ill will arises the desire for ill will; dependent on the desire for ill will arises the fever of ill will; dependent on the fever of ill will arises the search for ill will. When engaged in a search for ill will, |bhikkhus,::::| the uninstructed ordinary person practices improperly in three ways: by way of body, speech, and mind.

Dependent on the element of harm, |bhikkhus,::::| there arises the perception of harm; dependent on the perception of harm arises the intention of harm; dependent on the intention of harm arises the desire for harm; dependent on the desire for harm arises the fever of harm; dependent on the fever of harm arises the search for harm. When engaged in a search for harm, |bhikkhus,::::| the uninstructed ordinary person practices improperly in three ways: by way of body, speech, and mind.

Just as|, bhikkhus,::::| a person might throw a burning grass torch into a thicket of dry grass, and if they did not quickly extinguish it with their hands and feet, then, |bhikkhus,::::| those living beings dependent on grass and wood would meet with misfortune and disaster.

In the same way, |bhikkhus,::::| whoever, whether an ascetic or a brahmin, does not quickly abandon, dispel, eliminate, and obliterate an arisen unwholesome perception, dwells in suffering in this very life with distress, anguish, and fever; and after the breaking up of the body, after death, a bad destination is to be expected.

A |thought of renunciation::thought of letting go of sensual pleasure [nekkhammavitakka]| arises from a cause, |bhikkhus,::::| not without a cause; a |thought of goodwill::thought of kindness, friendliness, benevolence [abyāpādavitakka]| arises from a cause, not without a cause; a |thought of non-harm::thought of helping, thought of non-cruelty [avihiṃsāvitakka]| arises from a cause, not without a cause.

And how|, bhikkhus,::::| does a thought of renunciation arise from a cause, not without a cause? How does a thought of goodwill arise from a cause, not without a cause? How does a thought of non-harm arise from a cause, not without a cause? Dependent on the element of renunciation, |bhikkhus,::::| there arises the perception of renunciation; dependent on the perception of renunciation arises the intention of renunciation; dependent on the intention of renunciation arises the desire for renunciation; dependent on the desire for renunciation arises the fever of renunciation; dependent on the fever of renunciation arises the search for renunciation. When engaged in a search for renunciation, |bhikkhus,::::| the |learned::well-studied, instructed [sutavant]| disciple of the Noble Ones practices properly in three ways: by way of body, speech, and mind.

Dependent on the element of goodwill, |bhikkhus,::::| there arises the perception of goodwill; dependent on the perception of goodwill arises the intention of goodwill; dependent on the intention of goodwill arises the desire for goodwill; dependent on the desire for goodwill arises the fever of goodwill; dependent on the fever of goodwill arises the search for goodwill. When engaged in a search for goodwill, |bhikkhus,::::| the learned disciple of the Noble Ones practices properly in three ways: by way of body, speech, and mind.

Dependent on the element of non-harm, |bhikkhus,::::| there arises the perception of non-harm; dependent on the perception of non-harm arises the intention of non-harm; dependent on the intention of non-harm arises the desire for non-harm; dependent on the desire for non-harm arises the fever of non-harm; dependent on the fever of non-harm arises the search for non-harm. When engaged in a search for non-harm, |bhikkhus,::::| the learned disciple of the Noble Ones practices properly in three ways: by way of body, speech, and mind.

Just as|, bhikkhus,::::| a person might throw a burning grass torch into a thicket of dry grass, and were to quickly extinguish it with their hands and feet, then, |bhikkhus,::::| those living beings dependent on grass and wood would not meet with misfortune and disaster.

In the same way, |bhikkhus,::::| whoever, whether an ascetic or a brahmin, quickly abandons, dispels, eliminates, and obliterates an arisen unwholesome perception, dwells happily in this very life without distress, without anguish, without fever; and after the breaking up of the body, after death, a good destination is to be expected.”
