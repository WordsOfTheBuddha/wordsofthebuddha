At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling at Sāvatthi, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park. At that time, the venerable |Girimānanda::name of an arahant monk; lit. happy mountain [girimānanda]| was sick, experiencing pain, and gravely ill. Then the venerable Ānanda approached the Blessed One; having approached, he paid homage to the Blessed One and sat down to one side. Sitting to one side, the venerable Ānanda said to the Blessed One:

“Venerable sir, the venerable Girimānanda is sick, experiencing pain, and gravely ill. It would be good, venerable sir, if the Blessed One would approach the venerable Girimānanda out of compassion.”

“If, Ānanda, you would speak about ten perceptions to the bhikkhu Girimānanda, it is possible that having heard these ten perceptions, his affliction would immediately subside.

#### Ten Perceptions

What ten? Perception of impermanence, perception of not-self, perception of unattractiveness, perception of drawback, perception of giving up, perception of fading of desire, perception of cessation, perception of non-delight in the whole world, perception of lack of desire for all constructs, mindfulness while breathing in and out.

1.) And what, Ānanda, is the |perception of impermanence::perception of instability, inconsistency [aniccasaññā]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu, having gone to the forest, to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, reflects thus: ‘|Form::materiality, material existence, experience of the material world, i.e. encompassing both one’s body and external objects, whether near or far, gross or subtle, deficient or refined; first of the five aggregates [rūpa]| is impermanent, |feeling::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, the experience felt on contact through eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind; second of the five aggregates [vedanā]| is impermanent, |perception::The mental process of recognizing and giving meaning to experience. It marks sensory information by signs, labels, or associations drawn from memory and the field of contact. Perception shapes how one experiences the world; third of the five aggregates [saññā]| is impermanent, |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]| are impermanent, |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object [viññāṇa]| is impermanent.’ Thus he dwells observing impermanence in these |five aggregates subject to clinging::the physical and mental heaps that are appropriated, grasped at, or taken as self; the fivefold collection of form, feeling, perception, intentional constructs, and consciousness bound up with attachment [pañca + upādānakkhandha]|. This is called, Ānanda, the perception of impermanence.

2.) And what, Ānanda, is the |perception of not-self::recognition of impersonality [anattasaññā]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu, having gone to the forest, to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, reflects thus: ‘The eye is not-self, forms are not-self; the ear is not-self, sounds are not-self; the nose is not-self, odors are not-self; the tongue is not-self, tastes are not-self; the body is not-self, tangible objects are not-self; the mind is not-self, mind objects are not-self.’ Thus he dwells observing not-self in these six internal and external sense bases. This is called, Ānanda, the perception of not-self.

3.) And what, Ānanda, is the perception of |unattractiveness::disagreeableness, recognizing the not aesthetically pleasing characteristics [asubha]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu reviews this very body from the soles of the feet upwards and from the top of the hair downwards, bounded by skin and full of various impurities thus: ‘In this body, there are hairs of the head, hairs of the body, nails, teeth, skin; flesh, sinews, bones, bone marrow, kidneys; heart, liver, inner membrane, spleen, lungs; intestines, mesentery, stomach contents, feces, [brain]; bile, phlegm, pus, blood, sweat, fat, tears, grease, saliva, nasal mucus, synovial fluid, and urine.’ Thus he dwells **observing unattractiveness in this body**.^[1]^ This is called, Ānanda, the perception of unattractiveness.

4.) And what, Ānanda, is the perception of |drawback::disadvantage, unsatisfactoriness, inadequacy, danger [ādīnava]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu, having gone to the forest, to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, reflects thus: ‘This body [is subject to] much suffering and with lots of drawbacks. Various |afflictions::disease, sickness, illness; lit. oppression [ābādhā]| arise in this body, namely: eye disease, ear disease, nose disease, tongue disease, body disease, head disease, external ear disease, mouth disease, tooth disease, lip disease; cough, asthma, catarrh, burning, fever, stomach ache, fainting, dysentery, colic, cholera, leprosy, boil, eczema, tuberculosis, epilepsy, ringworm, itch, scabies, nail disease, psoriasis, blood disease, bile disease, diabetes, hemorrhoids, pimple, fistula; illnesses originating from bile, illnesses originating from phlegm, illnesses originating from wind, illnesses originating from a conflict of humors, illnesses originating from changes in weather, illnesses originating from improper care, illnesses originating from assault, illnesses originating from the result of kamma; cold, heat, hunger, thirst, defecation, and urination.’ Thus he dwells observing drawback in this body. This is called, Ānanda, the perception of drawback.

5.) And what, Ānanda, is the perception of |giving up::letting go, abandoning, removing [pahāna]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu does not tolerate an arisen |thought of sensuality::thought related to enjoyment of sensual pleasures, sexual thought [kāmavitakka]|; he abandons it, dispels it, puts an end to it, and obliterates it. He does not tolerate an arisen |thought of ill will::intentional thought of mental rejection, hostility, or malevolence toward others; deliberate contemplation rooted in antipathy or wishing harm [byāpādavitakka]|; he abandons it, dispels it, puts an end to it, and obliterates it. He does not tolerate an arisen |thought of harming::thought of cruelty, violence, or aggression [vihiṃsāvitakka]|; he abandons it, dispels it, puts an end to it, and obliterates it. He does not tolerate any arisen unwholesome state; he abandons it, dispels it, puts an end to it, and obliterates it. This is called, Ānanda, the perception of giving up.

6.) And what, Ānanda, is the perception of |fading of desire::dispassion, detachment [virāga]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu, having gone to the forest, to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, reflects thus: ‘This is peaceful, this is sublime, namely |the stilling of all intentional constructs::calming of all intentions, volitions, mental activities. This is sometimes rendered as stilling of all formations [sabbasaṅkhārasamatha]|, the |relinquishing of all acquisitions::letting go of all attachments, releasing of all ‘I’, ‘me’, ‘mine’ making [sabbūpadhipaṭinissagga]|, the |wearing away of craving::depletion of craving, wanting, yearning, passion [taṇhākkhaya]|, fading of desire, |Nibbāna::complete cooling, letting go of everything, deathless, freedom from calamity, the non-disintegrating; lit. blowing away [nibbāna]|.’ This is called, Ānanda, the perception of fading of desire.

7.) And what, Ānanda, is the perception of |cessation::ending, termination [nirodha]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu, having gone to the forest, to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, reflects thus: ‘This is peaceful, this is sublime, namely the stilling of all intentional constructs, the relinquishing of all acquisitions, the wearing away of craving, cessation, Nibbāna.’ This is called, Ānanda, the perception of cessation.

8.) And what, Ānanda, is the |perception of non-delight::recognition of disenchantment with [anabhiratisaññā]| in regard to the whole world? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu dwells refraining from any |clinging::grasping, acquiring, appropriating, taking possession, identifying; fuel for fire; lit. taking near [upādāna]|, |determinations, attachments, and underlying tendencies::prejudices, adherences, and dormant proclivities [adhiṭṭhānābhinivesānusaya]| in regard to the whole world, abandoning them without clinging to them. This is called, Ānanda, the perception of non-delight in regard to the whole world.

9.) And what, Ānanda, is the **|perception of lack of desire::recognition of freedom from need [anicchāsaññā]|** ^[2]^ for all constructs? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu is horrified, repelled, and disgusted by all constructs. This is called, Ānanda, the perception of lack of desire for all constructs.

#### Mindfulness while breathing in and out

10.) And what, Ānanda, is |mindfulness while breathing in and out::mindfulness during inhalation and exhalation [ānāpānassati]|? Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu, having gone to the forest, or to the foot of a tree, or to an empty hut, sits down; having folded his legs crosswise, aligns his body upright, and establishes mindfulness |as his primary focal point::as first priority, directed towards the meditation object; lit. around the front [parimukhaṁ]|. Just mindful, he breathes in; just mindful, he breathes out.

##### Mindfulness of Body

1.) Breathing in long, he |discerns::distinguishes, understands, knows clearly [pajānāti]| the long in-breath; breathing out long, he discerns the long out-breath.
2.) Breathing in short, he discerns the short in-breath; breathing out short, he discerns the short out-breath.
3.) He trains to |experience the whole body::be conscious of the whole body, be sensitive to the whole process [sabbakāyapaṭisaṃvedī]| while breathing in; he trains to experience the whole body while breathing out.
4.) He trains to |settle::calm, still [passambhayanta]| the |bodily constructs::bodily processes associated with breathing, specifically the in-and-out breath. It encompasses the physical movements and sensations that arise from the act of breathing. [kāyasaṅkhārā]| while breathing in; he trains to settle the bodily constructs while breathing out.

##### Mindfulness of Felt Experience

1.) He trains to |experience uplifting joy::heartfelt or intense joy, feeling of love, rapture lit. lovely feeling [pītipaṭisaṃvedī]| while breathing in; he trains to experience uplifting joy while breathing out.
2.) He trains to |experience ease::contentment, happiness, pleasant abiding [sukhapaṭisaṃvedī]| while breathing in; he trains to experience ease while breathing out.
3.) He trains to |experience mental constructs::be sensitive to the mental processes; (comm) conscious of feeling and perception [cittasaṅkhārapaṭisaṃvedī]| while breathing in; he trains to experience mental constructs while breathing out.
4.) He trains to settle the mental constructs while breathing in; he trains to settle the mental constructs while breathing out.

If, Ānanda, you would speak about these ten perceptions to the bhikkhu Girimānanda, it is possible that having heard these ten perceptions, his affliction would immediately subside.”

Then the venerable Ānanda, having learned these ten perceptions in the presence of the Blessed One, approached the venerable Girimānanda; having approached, he spoke these ten perceptions to the venerable Girimānanda. Then, having heard these ten perceptions, the affliction of the venerable Girimānanda immediately subsided. The venerable Girimānanda recovered from that affliction, and in this way, the venerable Girimānanda was cured of his affliction.
