“In the past, |bhikkhus,::::| there was a teacher named Araka, a founder of a spiritual sect who was free from desire for sensual pleasures. Now, |bhikkhus,::::| the teacher Araka had many hundreds of disciples. The teacher Araka taught the Dhamma to his disciples thus: ‘Brahmin, the life of human beings is short, limited, and fleeting, with much |suffering::discomfort, unpleasantness, discontentment, dissatisfaction, stress, pain, disease, i.e. mild or intense suffering [dukkha]| and |despair::lit. many problems [bahupāyāsa]|. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life, for there is no immortality for one who is born.

Just as, brahmin, a dewdrop on the tip of a blade of grass quickly disperses when the sun rises and does not last long; in the same way, brahmin, the life of human beings is like a dewdrop: limited and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life, for there is no immortality for one who is born.

Just as, brahmin, when heavy rain is falling, a water bubble quickly bursts and does not last long; in the same way, brahmin, the life of human beings is like a water bubble: limited and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life, for there is no immortality for one who is born.

Just as, brahmin, a line etched with a stick on water quickly disappears and does not last long; in the same way, brahmin, the life of human beings is like a line etched on water: limited <collapse>and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life,</collapse> for there is no immortality for one who is born.

Just as, brahmin, a mountain river flowing far, with a swift current, carrying away everything in its path, has no moment, instant, or second when it pauses, but instead just flows on, rolls on, and surges forward; in the same way, brahmin, the life of human beings is like a mountain river: limited<collapse> and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life,</collapse> for there is no immortality for one who is born.

Just as, brahmin, a strong man might collect a ball of saliva at the tip of his tongue and spit it out without difficulty; in the same way, brahmin, the life of human beings is like a ball of saliva: limited <collapse>and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life,</collapse> for there is no immortality for one who is born.

Just as, brahmin, a piece of meat thrown into an iron pan that has been heated all day quickly sizzles away and does not last long; in the same way, brahmin, the life of human beings is like a piece of meat: limited <collapse>and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life,</collapse> for there is no immortality for one who is born.

Just as, brahmin, when a cow being led to slaughter lifts each foot, she comes closer to the slaughter, closer to death; in the same way, brahmin, the life of human beings is like a cow being led to slaughter: limited and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life, for there is no immortality for one who is born.’

Now on that occasion, |bhikkhus,::::| the lifespan of human beings was sixty thousand years, and a maiden of five hundred years was fit for marriage. On that occasion, |bhikkhus,::::| human beings had only six afflictions: cold, heat, hunger, thirst, defecation, and urination. Yet, |bhikkhus,::::| the teacher Araka taught the Dhamma to his disciples even among human beings who were so long-lived, enduring so long, and with so few afflictions: ‘Brahmin, the life of human beings is short, limited, and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life, for there is no immortality for one who is born.’

Nowadays, |bhikkhus,::::| one speaking rightly would say: ‘The life of human beings is short, limited, and fleeting, with much suffering and despair. One should understand this with wisdom, one should do what is wholesome, one should live the spiritual life, for there is no immortality for one who is born.’ Nowadays, |bhikkhus,::::| one who lives long lives for a hundred years, or a little more or less. Living for a hundred years, one lives for just three hundred seasons: one hundred seasons of winter, one hundred seasons of summer, and one hundred seasons of the rains. Living for three hundred seasons, one lives for just twelve hundred months: four hundred months of winter, four hundred months of summer, and four hundred months of the rains. Living for twelve hundred months, one lives for just twenty-four hundred fortnights: eight hundred fortnights of winter, eight hundred fortnights of summer, and eight hundred fortnights of the rains. Living for twenty-four hundred fortnights, one lives for just thirty-six thousand nights: twelve thousand nights of winter, twelve thousand nights of summer, and twelve thousand nights of the rains. Living for thirty-six thousand nights, one eats just seventy-two thousand meals: twenty-four thousand meals in winter, twenty-four thousand meals in summer, and twenty-four thousand meals in the rains, including mother’s milk, and including interruptions to meals.

Here, these are the interruptions to meals: when angry one does not eat a meal; when suffering in pain one does not eat a meal; when sick one does not eat a meal; when observing the Uposatha one does not eat a meal; and when unable to obtain food one does not eat a meal. Thus, |bhikkhus,::::| for a human being with a lifespan of a hundred years, I have counted the lifespan, I have counted the span of life, I have counted the seasons, I have counted the years, I have counted the months, I have counted the fortnights, I have counted the nights, I have counted the days, I have counted the meals, and I have counted the interruptions to meals. Whatever should be done by a teacher seeking the welfare of his disciples, out of compassion, that I have done for you|, bhikkhus::::|. Here are the roots of trees|, bhikkhus::::|; here are the empty huts. Meditate, bhikkhus, do not be negligent. Do not be |regretful::remorseful; lit. remembering back negatively [vippaṭisārī]| later. This is our instruction to you.”
