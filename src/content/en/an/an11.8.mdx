---
title: Manasikāra sutta - Attention
description: Ānanda asks the Buddha whether a meditator can attain collectedness without attending to the six sense bases, elements, formless bases, or mental concepts, yet still attend. The Buddha explains that attention is directed solely to the stilling of intentional constructs, Nibbāna.
qualities: collectedness, ending, radical attention
theme: wisdom, cultivating discernment
slug: an11.8
character: Venerable Ānanda
priority: 1.2
---

Then the venerable Ānanda approached the Blessed One. Having drawn near, he paid homage to the Blessed One and sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the venerable Ānanda addressed the Blessed One:

“Venerable sir, could a bhikkhu obtain such a state of collectedness that he would not |attend::bringing-to-mind, focusing; lit. making in the mind [manasi karoti]| to the |eye::faculty of seeing, vision [cakkhu]|, nor attend to |forms::visible objects [rūpa]|, nor attend to the ear, nor attend to sounds, nor attend to the nose, nor attend to odors, nor attend to the tongue, nor attend to tastes, nor attend to the body, nor attend to tangible objects, nor attend to |earth::ground [pathavī]|, nor attend to water, nor attend to fire, nor attend to wind, nor attend to the |base of boundless space::field of boundless expanse, dimension of infinite space [ākāsānañcāyatana]|, nor attend to the |base of boundless consciousness::field of limitless awareness [viññāṇañcāyatana]|, nor attend to the |base of nothingness::field of awareness centered on the absence of any distinct “something" to grasp or hold onto [ākiñcaññāyatana]|, nor attend to the |base of neither perception nor non-perception::field of awareness of subtle mental activity that do not arise to the level of forming a perception [nevasaññānāsaññāyatana]|, nor attend to this world, nor attend to the other world, nor attend even to whatever is seen, heard, |sensed::smelled, tasted, felt, or thought [muta]|, cognized, attained, sought after, and pondered over by the mind—and yet he would still attend?”

“He could, Ānanda. A bhikkhu could obtain such a state of collectedness that he would not attend to the eye, nor attend to forms, nor attend to the ear, nor attend to sounds, nor attend to the nose, nor attend to odors, nor attend to the tongue, nor attend to tastes, nor attend to the body, nor attend to tangible objects, nor attend to earth, nor attend to water, nor attend to fire, nor attend to wind, nor attend to the base of boundless space, nor attend to the base of boundless consciousness, nor attend to the base of nothingness, nor attend to the base of neither perception nor non-perception, nor attend to this world, nor attend to the other world, nor attend even to whatever is seen, heard, sensed, cognized, attained, sought after, and pondered over by the mind—and yet he would still attend.”

“But how, venerable sir, could a bhikkhu obtain such a state of collectedness that he would not attend to the eye, nor attend to forms<collapse>, nor attend to the ear, nor attend to sounds, nor attend to the nose, nor attend to odors, nor attend to the tongue, nor attend to tastes, nor attend to the body, nor attend to tangible objects, nor attend to earth, nor attend to water, nor attend to fire, nor attend to wind, nor attend to the base of boundless space, nor attend to the base of boundless consciousness, nor attend to the base of nothingness, nor attend to the base of neither perception nor non-perception, nor attend to this world, nor attend to the other world,</collapse> nor attend even to whatever is seen, heard, sensed, cognized, attained, sought after, and pondered over by the mind—and yet he would still attend?”

“Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu attends thus: ‘This is peaceful, this is sublime, namely |the stilling of all intentional constructs::calming of all intentions, volitions, mental activities. This is sometimes rendered as stilling of all formations [sabbasaṅkhārasamatha]|, the |relinquishing of all acquisitions::letting go of all attachments, releasing of all ‘I’, ‘me’, ‘mine’ making [sabbūpadhipaṭinissagga]|, the |wearing away of craving::depletion of craving, wanting, yearning, passion [taṇhākkhaya]|, the fading of desire, cessation, |Nibbāna::complete cooling, letting go of everything, deathless, freedom from calamity, the non-disintegrating; lit. blowing away [nibbāna]|.’

It is in this way, Ānanda, that a bhikkhu could obtain such a state of collectedness that he would not attend to the eye, nor attend to forms<collapse>, nor attend to the ear, nor attend to sounds, nor attend to the nose, nor attend to odors, nor attend to the tongue, nor attend to tastes, nor attend to the body, nor attend to tangible objects, nor attend to earth, nor attend to water, nor attend to fire, nor attend to wind, nor attend to the base of boundless space, nor attend to the base of boundless consciousness, nor attend to the base of nothingness, nor attend to the base of neither perception nor non-perception, nor attend to this world, nor attend to the other world,</collapse> nor attend even to whatever is seen, heard, sensed, cognized, attained, sought after, and pondered over by the mind—and yet he would still attend.”
