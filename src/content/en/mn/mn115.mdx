Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling at Sāvatthi, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park. There, the Blessed One addressed the bhikkhus: “Bhikkhus.”

“Venerable sir,” those bhikkhus replied to the Blessed One. The Blessed One said this:

### Dangers Arise from the Immature

“|Bhikkhus,::::| whatever |dangers::fears, perils [bhaya]| arise, all of them arise from the |immature person::lacking in discernment or good sense, child-like in understanding [bāla]|, not from the |wise::astute, intelligent, learned, skilled [paṇḍita]|; whatever misfortunes arise, all of them arise from the immature person, not from the wise; whatever troubles arise, all of them arise from the immature person, not from the wise. Just as|, bhikkhus,::::| a fire that has spread from a reed hut or a grass hut burns even gabled mansions that are plastered and painted, sheltered from the wind, with well-fitted doors and shuttered windows; so too, |bhikkhus,::::| whatever dangers arise, all of them arise from the immature person, not from the wise; whatever misfortunes arise, all of them arise from the immature person, not from the wise; whatever troubles arise, all of them arise from the immature person, not from the wise. Thus, |bhikkhus,::::| the immature person is a source of danger, the wise person is free from danger; the immature person is a source of misfortune, the wise person is free from misfortune; the immature person is a source of trouble, the wise person is free from trouble. There is|, bhikkhus,::::| no danger from the wise, no misfortune from the wise, no trouble from the wise. Therefore, |bhikkhus,::::| you should train yourselves thus: ‘We will be wise, we will be |inquirers::those who examine, investigate, inquire [vīmaṁsaka]|.’ Thus|, bhikkhus,::::| you should train yourselves.”

### Wise Inquirer

When this was said, the venerable Ānanda said to the Blessed One: “To what extent, venerable sir, can a bhikkhu be called wise and an inquirer?”

“When, Ānanda, a bhikkhu is skilled in the |elements::constituent properties, natural qualities [dhātu]|, skilled in the bases, skilled in |dependent co-arising::the process of arising together from a cause, chain of causation, dependent origination [paṭiccasamuppāda]|, and skilled in what is possible and what is impossible, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called wise and an inquirer.”

#### Skilled in the Elements

“And to what extent, venerable sir, can a bhikkhu be called ‘skilled in the elements’?”

“There are, Ānanda, these eighteen elements—the |eye element::faculty of vision [cakkhudhātu]|, the |form element::objects of vision, materials, including the realm and objects of subtle materiality [rūpadhātu]|, and the |eye-consciousness element::visual awareness [cakkhuviññāṇadhātu]|; the |ear element::hearing faculty [sotadhātu]|, the |sound element::auditory element, sounds, noises, voices [saddadhātu]|, and the |ear-consciousness element::auditory awareness [sotaviññāṇadhātu]|; the |nose element::faculty of perceiving odors [ghānadhātu]|, the |odor element::smells, scents, aroma, fragrances [gandhadhātu]|, and the |nose-consciousness element::awareness of smells [ghānaviññāṇadhātu]|; the |tongue element::faculty of perceiving taste [jivhādhātu]|, the |taste element::tastes, flavors [rasadhātu]|, and the |tongue-consciousness element::awareness of tastes [jivhāviññāṇadhātu]|; the |body element::faculty of perceiving tactile sensations [kāyadhātu]|, the |tangible object element::the elemental basis for touch sensation—such as hardness, softness, warmth, coolness, pressure, movement, or contact with textures and bodies—anything that, when contacted by the body, can give rise to tactile experience and potentially become an object of desire, arousal, comfort, or emotional attachment [phoṭṭhabbadhātu]|, and the |body-consciousness element::awareness of physical felt experiences [kāyaviññāṇadhātu]|; the |mind element::mentality [manodhātu]|, the |mental object element::the elemental basis for mental phenomena—such as thoughts, emotions, perceptions, volitions, conceptual constructs, and internal representations—that arise within the mind and form the condition for knowing, imagining, interpreting, or craving [dhammadhātu]|, and the |mind-consciousness element::knowing, mental awareness [manoviññāṇadhātu]|. When he knows and sees these eighteen elements, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements.’”

“But, venerable sir, could there be another way by which a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements’?”

“There could be, Ānanda. There are, Ānanda, these six elements—the |earth element::whatever internal or external that is solid, hard, resistant, appears stable and supporting, which can be considered as belonging to oneself, and can be clung to [pathavīdhātu]|, the |water element::whatever internal or external, that is liquid, cohesive, flowing, binding, moist, which can be considered as belonging to oneself, and can be clung to [āpodhātu]|, the |fire element::whatever internal or external that is hot, fiery, transformative, warming, cooling, which can be considered as belonging to oneself and can be clung to [tejodhātu]|, the |wind element::whatever internal or external that is airy, gaseous, moving, vibrating, wind-like, which can be considered as belonging to oneself and can be clung to [vāyodhātu]|, the |space element::whatever internal or external that is open, spacious, unobstructed, accommodating, which can be considered as belonging to oneself and can be clung to [ākāsadhātu]|, and the |consciousness element::whatever internal or external that is knowing, cognizing, recognizing, which can be considered as belonging to oneself and can be clung to [viññāṇadhātu]|. When he knows and sees these six elements, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements.’”

“But, venerable sir, could there be another way by which a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements’?”

“There could be, Ānanda. There are, Ānanda, these six elements—the |pleasure element::bodily pleasant feeling [sukhadhātu]|, the |pain element::bodily painful feeling [dukkhadhātu]|, the |mental pleasure element::agreeable feeling born of mental contact, mental happiness [somanassadhātu]|, the |mental displeasure element::disagreeable feeling or unease born of mental contact, mental pain [domanassadhātu]|, the |equanimity element::neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, mental poise [upekkhādhātu]|, and the |ignorance element::fundamental unawareness or misunderstanding of the true nature of reality, not experientially understanding the four noble truths [avijjādhātu]|. When he knows and sees these six elements, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements.’”

“But, venerable sir, could there be another way by which a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements’?”

“There could be, Ānanda. There are, Ānanda, these six elements—the |sensuality element::element of sensual desire; lit. sensual state [kāmadhātu]|, the |renunciation element::state of renunciation [nekkhammadhātu]|, the |ill will element::state of ill will [byāpādadhātu]|, the |non-ill will element::state of good will [abyāpādadhātu]|, the |cruelty element::state of harming [vihiṁsādhātu]|, and the |non-cruelty element::element of non-harming [avihiṁsādhātu]|. When he knows and sees these six elements, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements.’”

“But, venerable sir, could there be another way by which a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements’?”

“There could be, Ānanda. There are, Ānanda, these three elements—the sensuality element, the |form element::subtle material state [rūpadhātu]|, and the |formless element::states not rooted in materiality, including mental realms and meditative attainments beyond physical form [arūpadhātu]|. When he knows and sees these three elements, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements.’”

“But, venerable sir, could there be another way by which a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements’?”

“There could be, Ānanda. There are, Ānanda, these two elements—the |conditioned element::constructed, created, fabricated [saṅkhatādhātu]| and the |unconditioned element::not created, unconstructed, unformed, epithet of Nibbāna [asaṅkhatādhātu]|. When he knows and sees these two elements, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the elements.’”

#### Skilled in the Bases

“And to what extent, venerable sir, can a bhikkhu be called ‘skilled in the bases’?”

“There are, Ānanda, these six internal and external bases—the eye and forms, the ear and sounds, the nose and odors, the tongue and flavors, the body and tactile sensations, the mind and phenomena. When he knows and sees these six internal and external bases, to this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in the bases.’”

#### Skilled in Dependent Co-arising

“And to what extent, venerable sir, can a bhikkhu be called ‘skilled in dependent co-arising’?”

“Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu knows clearly: ‘When this exists, that comes to be; from the arising of this, that arises; when this does not exist, that does not come to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases, that is—|dependent on::contingent on, supported by, grounded on [paṭicca]| |ignorance::fundamental unawareness or misunderstanding of the true nature of reality, not experientially understanding the four noble truths [avijjā]|, |intentional constructs::the constructive activity that shapes each moment of experience, expressed as bodily, verbal, and mental formations; the accumulated conditioning — patterns, tendencies, and habits — produced by prior action [saṅkhārā]| [arise]; dependent on intentional constructs, |consciousness::quality of awareness — distinctive knowing that arises in dependence on the meeting of eye and form, ear and sound, nose and odor, tongue and taste, body and tangible object, mind and mind object [viññāṇa]| arises; dependent on consciousness, |name and form::mentality and materiality—the integrated structure of mental capacities (intention, attention, contact, feeling, perception) and physical form that together constitute and sustain an individual being [nāmarūpa]| arise; dependent on name and form, the |six sense bases::The six internal sense bases—eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, and mind—are the faculties that enable sensory contact and experience. They are not the physical organs themselves, but the functional conditions that enable consciousness to meet an object [saḷāyatana]| arise; dependent on the six sense bases, |contact::sense impingement, raw experience, touch [phassa]| arises; dependent on contact, |felt experience::pleasant, neutral, or painful sensation, feeling, second of the five aggregates [vedanā]| arises; dependent on felt experience, |craving::wanting, yearning, longing, attachment, lit. thirst [taṇha]| arises; dependent on craving, |clinging::grasping, acquiring, appropriating, taking possession, identifying; fuel for fire; lit. taking near [upādāna]| arises; dependent on clinging, |existence::being, becoming; continued conditional existence, the karmically conditioned mode of being that leads to future rebirth [bhava]| arises; dependent on existence, |birth::rebirth, conception, coming into existence [jāti]| arises; dependent on birth, aging and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair arise. Thus is the arising of this entire heap of suffering. With the |remainderless fading away and cessation::complete fading away and ending [asesavirāganirodha]| of ignorance comes the ending of intentional constructs; with the ending of intentional constructs, ending of consciousness; with the ending of consciousness, ending of name and form; with the ending of name and form, ending of the six sense bases; with the ending of the six sense bases, ending of contact; with the ending of contact, ending of felt experience; with the ending of felt experience, ending of craving; with the ending of craving, ending of clinging; with the ending of clinging, ending of existence; with the ending of existence, ending of birth; with the ending of birth, aging and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure, and despair cease. Thus is the ending of this entire heap of suffering.’ To this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in dependent co-arising.’”

#### Skilled in What is Possible and Impossible

“And to what extent, venerable sir, can a bhikkhu be called ‘skilled in what is possible and what is impossible’?”

“Here, Ānanda, a bhikkhu knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person |accomplished in view::with correct outlook, attitude, belief [diṭṭhisampanna]| could consider any |conditioned phenomenon::fabrication, concoction, intentional construct, karmic activity [saṅkhāra]| as |permanent::stable, not in flux [nicca]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an |ordinary person::common person, worldling [puthujjana]| might consider some conditioned phenomenon as permanent. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view could consider any conditioned phenomenon as |pleasurable::pleasant, contentment, happiness, ease [sukha]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person might consider some conditioned phenomenon as pleasurable. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view could consider any phenomenon as |personal::related to oneself, self, essence [atta]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person might consider some phenomenon as personal. Such a possibility does exist.’

He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view could deprive their mother of life. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person might deprive their mother of life. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view could deprive their father of life. Such a possibility does not exist.’ <collapse>He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person might deprive their father of life. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view could deprive an |Arahant::a worthy one, a fully awakened being, epithet of the Buddha [arahant]| of life. Such a possibility does not exist.’</collapse> He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person might deprive an arahant of life. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view, with a |corrupted mind::corrupted state of mind, such as having anger, being upset, or being resentful [duṭṭhacitta]|, could cause the |Tathāgata::one who has arrived at the truth, an epithet of a perfectly Awakened One [tathāgata]| to bleed. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person with a corrupted mind might cause the Tathāgata to bleed. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view could |cause a schism in the Saṅgha::cause a splitting apart of the monastic community [saṅgha bhindati]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person might cause a schism in the Saṅgha. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a person accomplished in view could acknowledge someone other [than the Buddha] as their teacher. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an ordinary person might acknowledge someone other [than the Buddha] as their teacher. Such a possibility does exist.’

He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that two arahants who are perfectly Awakened Ones could arise at the same time in one world system. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one arahant who is a |perfectly Awakened One::fully awakened being, fully enlightened being [sammāsambuddha]| might arise in one world system. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that two wheel-turning monarchs could arise at the same time in one world system. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one |wheel-turning monarch::a just and virtuous ruler whose domain extends over the entire known world [rājā cakkavattī]| might arise in one world system. Such a possibility does exist.’

He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a woman could be an arahant, a perfectly Awakened One. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that a man could be an arahant, a perfectly Awakened One. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a woman could be a wheel-turning monarch. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that a man could be a wheel-turning monarch. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a woman could occupy the position of |Sakka::name of the king of the deities [sakka]|, of |Māra::the ruler of the sensual realm, often depicted as a tempter who tries to obstruct beings from the path to liberation [māra]|, or of |Brahmā::God, the first deity to be born at the beginning of a new cosmic cycle and whose lifespan lasts for the entire cycle [brahmā]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that a man could occupy the position of Sakka, of Māra, or of Brahmā. Such a possibility does exist.’

He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a wished for, desirable, or agreeable result could be produced from |bodily misconduct::killing living beings, harming others, theft, or sexual misconduct [kāyaduccarita]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an unwished for, undesirable, or disagreeable result could be produced from bodily misconduct. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a wished for, desirable, or agreeable result could be produced from |verbal misconduct::false speech, harsh speech, divisive speech, idle chatter [vacīduccarita]|. <collapse>Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that a wished for, desirable, or agreeable result could be produced from</collapse> |mental misconduct::yearning with strong eagerness and desire, having ill will or hatred, delighting in harm [manoduccarita]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an unwished for, undesirable, or disagreeable result could be produced from verbal misconduct. <collapse>Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that an unwished for, undesirable, or disagreeable result could be produced from</collapse> mental misconduct. Such a possibility does exist.’

He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that an unwished for, undesirable, or disagreeable result could be produced from good bodily conduct. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that a wished for, desirable, or agreeable result could be produced from good bodily conduct. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that an unwished for, undesirable, or disagreeable result could be produced from |good verbal conduct::speaking at proper time, being truthful, gentle, beneficial and speaking with a mind of loving-kindness [vacīsucarita]|. <collapse>Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that an unwished for, undesirable, or disagreeable result could be produced from</collapse> |good mental conduct::thoughts of generosity, letting go, thoughts of non-ill will, benevolence, thoughts of non-harm, compassion [manosucarita]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that a wished for, desirable, or agreeable result could be produced from good verbal conduct. <collapse>Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that a wished for, desirable, or agreeable result could be produced from</collapse> good mental conduct. Such a possibility does exist.’

He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that one who engages in bodily misconduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a good destination, in a |heavenly world::a blissful realm, celestial world [sagga]|. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one who engages in bodily misconduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a state of loss, in a bad destination, in the lower realms, in |hell::a place of intense suffering, lit. no good fortune [niraya]|. Such a possibility does exist.’

He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that one who engages in verbal misconduct, <collapse>on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a good destination, in a heavenly world. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that one who engages in</collapse> mental misconduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a good destination, in a heavenly world. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one who engages in verbal misconduct, <collapse>on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a state of loss, in a bad destination, in the lower realms, in hell. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one who engages in</collapse> mental misconduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a state of loss, in a bad destination, in the lower realms, in hell. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that one who engages in good bodily conduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a state of loss, in a bad destination, in the lower realms, in hell. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one who engages in good bodily conduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a good destination, in the heavenly world. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that one who engages in good verbal conduct<collapse>, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a state of loss, in a bad destination, in the lower realms, in hell. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘It is impossible, it is inconceivable that one who engages in </collapse>good mental conduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a state of loss, in a bad destination, in the lower realms, in hell. Such a possibility does not exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one who engages in good verbal conduct, <collapse>on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a good destination, in the heavenly world. Such a possibility does exist.’ He knows clearly: ‘But it is possible that one who engages in</collapse> good mental conduct, on account of that, due to that condition, after the breaking up of the body at death, could be reborn in a good destination, in the heavenly world. Such a possibility does exist.’ To this extent, Ānanda, a bhikkhu can be called ‘skilled in what is possible and what is impossible.’”

When this was said, the venerable Ānanda said to the Blessed One: “It is wonderful, venerable sir, it is marvelous! Venerable sir, what is the name of this Dhamma discourse?”

“As to that, Ānanda, you may remember this Dhamma discourse as ‘The Many Kinds of Elements’; you may remember it as ‘The Fourfold Cycle’; you may remember it as ‘The Mirror of the Dhamma’; you may remember it as ‘The Drum of the |Deathless::deathless state, epithet of Nibbāna [amata]|’; you may remember it as ‘The Unsurpassed Triumph in Battle.’”

The Blessed One said this. The venerable Ānanda was delighted and pleased with the Blessed One’s words.
