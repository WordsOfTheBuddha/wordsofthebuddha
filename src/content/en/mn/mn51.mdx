description: Prompted by an elephant trainer's observation on the tangled, deceitful nature of humans compared to animals, the Buddha categorizes humanity into four types based on who they torment. He then details the profound path of the fourth person—the one who abandons all harm to attain complete, unburdened liberation in this very life.

#### Four Kinds of Persons

“Here, Pessa, a certain person torments himself, being devoted to the practice of torturing oneself.

Here a certain person torments others, being devoted to the practice of torturing others.

Here a certain person torments himself and torments others, being devoted to the practice of torturing oneself and others.

Here a certain person neither torments himself nor torments others, not being devoted to the practice of torturing oneself or others.

Of these four persons, Pessa, which one pleases your mind?”

“Venerable sir, the person who torments himself, devoted to the practice of torturing oneself, does not please my mind. The person who torments others, devoted to the practice of torturing others, does not please my mind. The person who torments himself and torments others, devoted to the practice of torturing oneself and others, does not please my mind. But the person who neither torments himself nor others, who in this very life is satiated, quenched, become cool, experiences ease, and abides with a purified mind like the Brahmā—this person alone pleases my mind.”

“Why, Pessa, do those first three persons not please your mind?”

“Venerable sir, the person who torments himself tortures and burns himself even though he desires happiness and recoils from pain—that is why this person does not please my mind. The person who torments others tortures and burns others even though they desire happiness and recoil from pain—that is why this person does not please my mind. The person who torments both himself and others tortures and burns both himself and others even though they desire happiness and recoil from pain—that is why this person does not please my mind. But the person who neither torments himself nor others does not torture or burn either himself or others, who both desire happiness and recoil from pain—that is why this person pleases my mind. And now, venerable sir, we must depart; we are busy and have much to do.”

Thus, with the mind collected, purified, clarified, blemish-free, free from impurities, malleable, flexible, suitable for use, stable, and attained to imperturbability, he directs the mind toward the knowledge of the ending of the |taints::outflows, discharges; (comm) mental defilements [āsavā]|. He understands as it actually is, ‘This is |suffering::discomfort, unpleasantness, discontentment, dissatisfaction, stress, pain, disease, i.e. mild or intense suffering [dukkha]|,’ he understands as it actually is, ‘This is the |arising of suffering::source of stress, appearance of discomfort [dukkhasamudaya]|,’ he understands as it actually is, ‘This is the |end of suffering::ending of discontentment, cessation of distress [dukkhanirodha]|,’ he understands as it actually is, ‘This is the |way of practice leading to the end of suffering::i.e. the Noble Eightfold Path consisting of right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right collectedness [dukkhanirodhagāmī]|.’

He understands as it actually is, ‘These are the taints,’ he understands as it actually is, ‘This is the arising of taints,’ he understands as it actually is, ‘This is the end of taints,’ he understands as it actually is, ‘This is the way of practice leading to the end of taints.’ Knowing and seeing thus, his mind is liberated from the taint of sensual desire, the taint of becoming, and the taint of |ignorance::fundamental unawareness or misunderstanding of the true nature of reality, not experientially understanding the four noble truths [avijjā]|. In liberation, there arises the knowledge: ‘Liberated.’

He knows clearly: ‘Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of existence.’

This is called|, bhikkhus,::::| the person who neither torments himself nor is devoted to the practice of torturing oneself, nor torments others nor is devoted to the practice of torturing others—who, without tormenting oneself or others, in this very life is satiated, quenched, become cool, experiencing ease, and abides with a purified mind like the Brahmā.”

The Blessed One said this. The bhikkhus were delighted and pleased with the Blessed One’s words.
