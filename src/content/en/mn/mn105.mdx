title: Sunakkhatta sutta - With Sunakkhatta
description: Sunakkhatta asks whether bhikkhus who declared final knowledge truly awakened or spoke from overestimation. Using vivid similes, the Buddha traces five progressive inclinations up to Nibbāna, then contrasts premature conceit with the unshakable sense restraint of one freed from attachment.

Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling at |Vesāli::capital of the Licchavīs [vesālī]|, in the |Great Forest::name of a forest outside Vesāli; lit. great forest [mahāvana]|, in the hall with the peaked roof.

Now at that time, several bhikkhus had declared |final knowledge::spiritual insight of the complete wearing away of the taints; full awakening [aññā]| in the presence of the Blessed One:

“We know clearly: ‘Birth is exhausted, the |spiritual life::a life of celibacy, contemplation, and ethical discipline lived for the sake of liberation; oriented toward inner development rather than sensual pleasures [brahmacariya]| has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any |state of existence::state of being [itthatta]|.’”

Sunakkhatta, the son of the Licchavis, heard: “It is said that several bhikkhus have declared final knowledge in the presence of the Blessed One: ‘We know clearly: “Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of existence.”’”

Then Sunakkhatta, the son of the Licchavis, approached the Blessed One. Having drawn near and paid homage to the Blessed One, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, Sunakkhatta, the son of the Licchavis, said this to the Blessed One: “I have heard this, venerable sir: ‘It is said that several bhikkhus have declared final knowledge in the presence of the Blessed One: “We know clearly: ‘Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of existence.’”’ Venerable sir, regarding those bhikkhus who declared final knowledge in the presence of the Blessed One: ‘We know clearly: “Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of existence”’—did those bhikkhus declare final knowledge rightly, venerable sir, or are there some bhikkhus here who declared final knowledge out of |overestimation::conceit of attainment, imagining one has reached a state not yet attained [adhimāna]|?”

“Regarding those bhikkhus, Sunakkhatta, who declared final knowledge in my presence: ‘We know clearly: “Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of existence”’—there are some bhikkhus here who declared final knowledge rightly, and there are also some bhikkhus here who declared final knowledge out of overestimation. Therein, Sunakkhatta, when those bhikkhus declared final knowledge rightly, that is indeed true of them. When, however, those bhikkhus declared final knowledge out of overestimation, therein, Sunakkhatta, this thought occurs to the |Tathāgata::one who has arrived at the truth, an epithet of a perfectly Awakened One [tathāgata]|: ‘I shall teach them the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]|.’ Thus, Sunakkhatta, this thought occurs to the Tathāgata here: ‘I shall teach them the Dhamma.’ Yet some |misguided men::vain persons [moghapurisā]| here, having |formulated::constructed, prepared [abhisaṅkharitvā]| question after question, approach the Tathāgata and ask them. Therein, Sunakkhatta, even though this thought had occurred to the Tathāgata: ‘I shall teach them the Dhamma,’ |it changes::there is an alteration, a change of mind [aññathatta]|.”

“This is the time, Blessed One, this is the time, Accomplished One, for the Blessed One to teach the Dhamma. Having heard it from the Blessed One, the bhikkhus will remember it.”

“Then listen to this, Sunakkhatta, and pay close attention; I will speak.”

“Yes, venerable sir,” Sunakkhatta, the son of the Licchavis, replied to the Blessed One. The Blessed One said this:

#### Five Progressive Inclinations

“There are these five cords of sensual pleasure, Sunakkhatta. What five? |Forms::visible objects such as beautiful sights, faces, expressions, art, ornaments, possessions, status symbols, admired appearances, enticing scenery, or objects of desire and attachment [rūpe]| cognizable by the eye that are desirable, lovely, agreeable, pleasing, sensual, and |enticing::arousing, tantalizing [rajanīya]|; |sounds::auditory experiences such as speech, music, praise, blame, ambient noise, verbal expression, or melodic tones—any sound that can stir emotion, craving for praise, aesthetic delight, or attachment to communication and identity [saddā]| cognizable by the ear <collapse>that are desirable, lovely, agreeable, pleasing, sensual, and enticing;</collapse> |odors::smells and fragrances experienced through the nose, including perfumes, flowers, food aromas, earth after rain, incense, or even stench—any olfactory experience that may evoke craving, aversion, nostalgia, comfort, or sensual pleasure [gandhā]| cognizable by the nose <collapse>that are desirable, lovely, agreeable, pleasing, sensual, and enticing;</collapse> |tastes::flavors experienced through the tongue such as sweetness, bitterness, sourness, saltiness, spiciness, richness, or subtle tastes like umami or astringency—any gustatory experience that can become an object of craving, indulgence, comfort, or sensory delight [rasā]| cognizable by the tongue <collapse>that are desirable, lovely, agreeable, pleasing, sensual, and enticing;</collapse> |tangible objects::tangible contact such as the feel of skin, warmth, softness, intimacy, physical affection, or sensations like massage, breath, water, air, pressure—anything felt through the body that can become an object of desire, arousal, comfort, or emotional attachment [phoṭṭhabba]| cognizable by the body that are desirable, lovely, agreeable, pleasing, sensual, and enticing. These, Sunakkhatta, are the five cords of sensual pleasure.

It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that a certain individual here may be |intent on::focused on, dedicated to, resolved on [adhimutta]| the |world’s bait::material pleasures and gains of the world [lokāmisa]|. For an individual intent on the world’s bait, Sunakkhatta, conversation conforming to that |settles::takes hold, feels natural [saṇṭhāti]| in him, he |ponders over::reflects on, rethinks [anuvitakketi]| and |re-examines::relooks at [anuvicāreti]| in line with that, he associates with that kind of person, and he experiences |pleasure::happiness, satisfaction; lit. gain [vitti]| with him. But when talk connected with the |imperturbable::fourth jhāna and the lower formless attainments; lit. not moving state [āneñja]| is going on, he does not wish to listen, does not lend an ear, and does not apply his mind to understand; nor does he associate with that kind of person, nor does he experience pleasure with him.

Suppose, Sunakkhatta, a man had been away from his own village or town for a long time, and he were to see another man who had recently left that village or town. He would ask that man about the safety, abundance of food, and freedom from illness of that village or town, and that man would tell him about the safety, abundance of food, and freedom from illness of that village or town. What do you think, Sunakkhatta? Would that man wish to listen to him, lend an ear, and apply his mind to understand, and would he associate with that man and experience pleasure with him?”

“Yes, venerable sir.”

“In the same way, Sunakkhatta, it is possible that a certain individual here may be intent on the world’s bait. For an individual intent on the world’s bait, Sunakkhatta, conversation conforming to that settles in him, he ponders over and re-examines in line with that, he associates with that kind of person, and he experiences pleasure with him. But when talk connected with the imperturbable is going on, he does not wish to listen, does not lend an ear, and does not apply his mind to understand; nor does he associate with that kind of person, nor does he experience pleasure with him. He should be understood thus: ‘This individual intent on the world’s bait is |disentangled from::detached from, unfettered by [visaṁyutta]| the bond of the imperturbable.’

It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that a certain individual here may be intent on the imperturbable. For an individual intent on the imperturbable, Sunakkhatta, conversation conforming to that settles in him, he ponders over and re-examines in line with that, he associates with that kind of person, and he experiences pleasure with him. But when talk connected with the world’s bait is going on, he does not wish to listen, does not lend an ear, and does not apply his mind to understand; nor does he associate with that kind of person, nor does he experience pleasure with him.

Just as, Sunakkhatta, a withered leaf detached from its stem is incapable of becoming green again, so too, Sunakkhatta, for an individual intent on the imperturbable, the fetter of the world’s bait has fallen away. He should be understood thus: ‘This individual intent on the imperturbable is disentangled from the fetter of the world’s bait.’

It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that a certain individual here may be intent on the |base of nothingness::field of awareness centered on the absence of any distinct “something" to grasp or hold onto [ākiñcaññāyatana]|. For an individual intent on the base of nothingness, Sunakkhatta, conversation conforming to that settles in him, he ponders over and re-examines in line with that, he associates with that kind of person, and he experiences pleasure with him. But when talk connected with the imperturbable is going on, he does not wish to listen, does not lend an ear, and does not apply his mind to understand; nor does he associate with that kind of person, nor does he experience pleasure with him.

Just as, Sunakkhatta, a flat rock split in two cannot be joined back together again, so too, Sunakkhatta, for an individual intent on the base of nothingness, the fetter of the imperturbable has been broken. He should be understood thus: ‘This individual intent on the base of nothingness is disentangled from the fetter of the imperturbable.’

It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that a certain individual here may be intent on the |base of neither perception nor non-perception::field of awareness of subtle mental activity that do not arise to the level of forming a perception [nevasaññānāsaññāyatana]|. For an individual intent on the base of neither perception nor non-perception, Sunakkhatta, conversation conforming to that settles in him, he ponders over and re-examines in line with that, he associates with that kind of person, and he experiences pleasure with him. But when talk connected with the base of nothingness is going on, he does not wish to listen, does not lend an ear, and does not apply his mind to understand; nor does he associate with that kind of person, nor does he experience pleasure with him.

Suppose, Sunakkhatta, a man who had eaten delicious food were to vomit it up. What do you think, Sunakkhatta? Would that man have any desire to eat that food again?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, that food is considered repulsive.”

“In the same way, Sunakkhatta, for an individual intent on the base of neither perception nor non-perception, the fetter of the base of nothingness has been vomited up. He should be understood thus: ‘This individual intent on the base of neither perception nor non-perception is disentangled from the fetter of the base of nothingness.’

It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that a certain individual here may be rightly intent on |Nibbāna::complete cooling, letting go of everything, deathless, freedom from calamity, the non-disintegrating; lit. blowing away [nibbāna]|. For an individual rightly intent on Nibbāna, Sunakkhatta, conversation conforming to that settles in him, he ponders over and re-examines in line with that, he associates with that kind of person, and he experiences pleasure with him. But when talk connected with the base of neither perception nor non-perception is going on, he does not wish to listen, does not lend an ear, and does not apply his mind to understand; nor does he associate with that kind of person, nor does he experience pleasure with him.

Just as, Sunakkhatta, a palm tree with its crown cut off is incapable of further growth, so too, Sunakkhatta, for an individual rightly intent on Nibbāna, the fetter of the base of neither perception nor non-perception has been cut off at the root, made like a palm stump, obliterated, and is not subject to future arising. He should be understood thus: ‘This individual rightly intent on Nibbāna is disentangled from the fetter of the base of neither perception nor non-perception.’

#### Overestimation and the Poisoned Arrow

It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that it might occur to a certain bhikkhu here: ‘|Craving::wanting, yearning, longing, attachment, lit. thirst [taṇhā]| has been called an |arrow::(any sharp pointed object which causes pain) dart [salla]| by the Ascetic; the poisonous taint of |ignorance::fundamental unawareness or misunderstanding of the true nature of reality, not experientially understanding the four noble truths [avijjā]| |is aggravated::festers, is inflamed, is afflicted [ruppati]| by |desire::intention, wish, impulse, interest [chanda]|, |passion::intense desire, strong emotion, infatuation, obsession, lust [rāga]|, and |ill will::hostile thought, malevolent intent, wishing ruin or downfall [byāpāda]|. That arrow of craving has been abandoned by me, the poisonous taint of ignorance has been removed, and I am rightly intent on Nibbāna.’ He might have |such conceit::such self-estimation [evaṁmāni]|, though it is not true. He would pursue things that are |unsuitable::unconducive, unbeneficial [asappāya]| for one rightly intent on Nibbāna: he would pursue seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, he would pursue unsuitable sounds with the ear, he would pursue unsuitable odors with the nose, he would pursue unsuitable tastes with the tongue, he would pursue unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and he would pursue unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind. As he pursues seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, pursues unsuitable sounds with the ear, pursues unsuitable odors with the nose, pursues unsuitable tastes with the tongue, pursues unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and pursues unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind, |lust::passion, infatuation, desire [rāga]| |invades::corrupts, infects [anuddhaṁseti]| his mind. With his mind invaded by lust, he would meet with death or deadly |suffering::discomfort, unpleasantness, discontentment, dissatisfaction, stress, pain, disease, i.e. mild or intense suffering [dukkha]|.

Suppose, Sunakkhatta, a man were struck by an arrow thickly smeared with poison. His friends and companions, his |relatives::kinsmen [ñātī]| and |family members::blood relatives [sālohitā]|, would bring a |surgeon::doctor, physician [bhisakka]| to treat him. That surgeon would cut around the opening of the wound with a knife. Having cut around the opening of the wound with a knife, he would probe for the arrow with a probe. Having probed for the arrow with a probe, he would extract the arrow and remove the poisonous taint |with residue remaining::leaving a remnant behind [saupādisesa]|. Knowing that a residue remained, he would say: ‘Good man, your arrow has been drawn out, and the poisonous taint has been removed with residue remaining, though it is not enough to cause your ruin. Eat only suitable foods; do not eat unsuitable foods, lest the wound |fester::ooze, become infected [assāvī]|. Wash the wound from time to time and anoint its opening from time to time; do not fail to wash the wound from time to time and anoint its opening from time to time, lest pus and blood cover the opening of the wound. Do not go walking about in the wind and sun, lest dust and dirt infect the opening of the wound as you walk about in the wind and sun. Take care of the wound, good man, and see that the wound heals.’

Then this thought would occur to him: ‘My arrow has been drawn out, and the poisonous taint has been removed |without residue::leaving no remnant behind [anupādisesa]|; it is not enough to cause my ruin.’ He would eat unsuitable foods, and as he ate unsuitable foods, his wound would fester. He would neither wash the wound from time to time nor anoint its opening from time to time, and as he failed to wash the wound from time to time and anoint its opening from time to time, pus and blood would cover the opening of the wound. He would go walking about in the wind and sun, and as he walked about in the wind and sun, dust and dirt would infect the opening of the wound. He would neither take care of the wound nor see to its healing. For him, both because of doing what was unsuitable and because the foul poisonous taint had been removed with residue remaining, the wound would grow worse. With the wound grown worse, he would meet with death or deadly suffering.

In the same way, Sunakkhatta, it is possible that it might occur to a certain bhikkhu here: ‘Craving has been called an arrow by the Ascetic; the poisonous taint of ignorance is aggravated by desire, passion, and ill will. That arrow of craving has been abandoned by me, the poisonous taint of ignorance has been removed, and I am rightly intent on Nibbāna.’ He might have such conceit, though it is not true. He would pursue things that are unsuitable for one rightly intent on Nibbāna: he would pursue seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, he would pursue unsuitable sounds with the ear, he would pursue unsuitable odors with the nose, he would pursue unsuitable tastes with the tongue, he would pursue unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and he would pursue unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind. As he pursues seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, pursues unsuitable sounds with the ear, pursues unsuitable odors with the nose, pursues unsuitable tastes with the tongue, pursues unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and pursues unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind, lust invades his mind. With his mind invaded by lust, he would meet with death or deadly suffering.

For this, Sunakkhatta, is death in the Noble One’s |Vinaya::code of monastic discipline rules, training [vinaya]|: that one gives up the training and |reverts back to the household life::returns to lay-life, secular world [hīnāyāvattati]|. And this, Sunakkhatta, is deadly suffering: that one commits a certain |defiled::tainted, corrupted [saṅkiliṭṭha]| |offense::transgression, wrongdoing [āpatti]|.

#### Rightly Intent on Nibbāna

It is possible, Sunakkhatta, that it might occur to a certain bhikkhu here: ‘Craving has been called an arrow by the Ascetic; the poisonous taint of ignorance is aggravated by desire, passion, and ill will. That arrow of craving has been abandoned by me, the poisonous taint of ignorance has been removed, and I am rightly intent on Nibbāna.’ Being truly rightly intent on Nibbāna, he would not pursue things that are unsuitable for one rightly intent on Nibbāna: he would not pursue seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, he would not pursue unsuitable sounds with the ear, he would not pursue unsuitable odors with the nose, he would not pursue unsuitable tastes with the tongue, he would not pursue unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and he would not pursue unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind. As he does not pursue seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, does not pursue unsuitable sounds with the ear, does not pursue unsuitable odors with the nose, does not pursue unsuitable tastes with the tongue, does not pursue unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and does not pursue unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind, lust does not invade his mind. With his mind not invaded by lust, he would meet with neither death nor deadly suffering.

Suppose, Sunakkhatta, a man were struck by an arrow thickly smeared with poison. His friends and companions, his relatives and family members, would bring a surgeon to treat him. That surgeon would cut around the opening of the wound with a knife. Having cut around the opening of the wound with a knife, he would probe for the arrow with a probe. Having probed for the arrow with a probe, he would extract the arrow and remove the poisonous taint without residue. Knowing that no residue remained, he would say: ‘Good man, your arrow has been drawn out, and the poisonous taint has been removed without residue; it is not enough to cause your ruin. Eat only suitable foods; do not eat unsuitable foods, lest the wound fester. Wash the wound from time to time and anoint its opening from time to time. Do not fail to wash the wound from time to time and anoint its opening from time to time, lest pus and blood cover the opening of the wound. Do not go walking about in the wind and sun, lest dust and dirt infect the opening of the wound as you walk about in the wind and sun. Take care of the wound, good man, and see that the wound heals.’

Then this thought would occur to him: ‘My arrow has been drawn out, and the poisonous taint has been removed without residue; it is not enough to cause my ruin.’ He would eat suitable foods, and as he ate suitable foods, his wound would not fester. He would wash the wound from time to time and anoint its opening from time to time, and as he washed the wound from time to time and anointed its opening from time to time, pus and blood would not cover the opening of the wound. He would not go walking about in the wind and sun, and as he refrained from walking about in the wind and sun, dust and dirt would not infect the opening of the wound. He would take care of the wound and see to its healing. For him, both because of doing what was suitable and because that poisonous taint had been removed without residue, the wound would heal. With the wound healed and covered over with skin, he would meet with neither death nor deadly suffering.

In the same way, Sunakkhatta, it is possible that it might occur to a certain bhikkhu here: ‘Craving has been called an arrow by the Ascetic; the poisonous taint of ignorance is aggravated by desire, passion, and ill will. That arrow of craving has been abandoned by me, the poisonous taint of ignorance has been removed, and I am rightly intent on Nibbāna.’ Being truly rightly intent on Nibbāna, he would not pursue things that are unsuitable for one rightly intent on Nibbāna: he would not pursue seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, he would not pursue unsuitable sounds with the ear, he would not pursue unsuitable odors with the nose, he would not pursue unsuitable tastes with the tongue, he would not pursue unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and he would not pursue unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind. As he does not pursue seeing unsuitable forms with the eye, does not pursue unsuitable sounds with the ear, does not pursue unsuitable odors with the nose, does not pursue unsuitable tastes with the tongue, does not pursue unsuitable tangible objects with the body, and does not pursue unsuitable mental phenomena with the mind, lust does not invade his mind. With his mind not invaded by lust, he would meet with neither death nor deadly suffering.

#### Meaning of the Simile and Restraint of the Liberated One

I have given this simile, Sunakkhatta, to convey a meaning. And this is the meaning here: ‘Wound,’ Sunakkhatta, is a designation for the |six internal sense bases::six inner sense spheres, the sense faculties (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) [cha + ajjhattika + āyatana]|. ‘Poisonous taint,’ Sunakkhatta, is a designation for ignorance. ‘Arrow,’ Sunakkhatta, is a designation for craving. ‘Probe,’ Sunakkhatta, is a designation for |mindfulness::active presence of mind and lucid retention, recollection, keeping in mind [sati]|. ‘Knife,’ Sunakkhatta, is a designation for noble |wisdom::distinctive knowledge, discernment [paññā]|. ‘Surgeon,’ Sunakkhatta, is a designation for the Tathāgata, the |Arahant::a worthy one, a fully awakened being, epithet of the Buddha [arahant]|, the perfectly Awakened One.

Indeed, Sunakkhatta, that bhikkhu practices restraint in the |six fields of contact::six fields of experience, six sense bases [cha + phassāyatana]|. Having understood that ‘|acquisition::attachment, taking as mine, appropriation, sense of ownership, identification [upadhi]| is the root of suffering,’ being |without acquisitions::free from attachment, not taking as mine [nirupadhi]|, liberated through the |exhaustion of acquisitions::eradication of attachment, ending of identification [upadhisaṅkhaya]|, it is impossible that he would direct his body toward any acquisition or arouse his mind toward it.

Suppose, Sunakkhatta, there were a bronze cup of beverage—delightful in appearance, aromatic, and full of flavor—but it was mixed with poison. Then a man would come along, wishing to live, not wishing to die, desiring happiness, and |averse to suffering::opposed to pain [dukkhapaṭikūla]|. What do you think, Sunakkhatta? Would that man drink that bronze cup of beverage if he knew: ‘Having drunk this, I will meet with death or deadly suffering’?”

“No, venerable sir.”

“In the same way, Sunakkhatta, that bhikkhu practices restraint in the six fields of contact. Having understood that ‘acquisition is the root of suffering,’ being without acquisitions, liberated through the exhaustion of acquisitions, it is impossible that he would direct his body toward any acquisition or arouse his mind toward it.

Suppose, Sunakkhatta, there were a deadly |venomous snake::fiercely poisonous serpent, viper [āsīvisa + ghoravisa]|. Then a man would come along, wishing to live, not wishing to die, desiring happiness, and averse to suffering. What do you think, Sunakkhatta? Would that man offer his hand or his thumb to that deadly venomous snake if he knew: ‘Bitten by this, I will meet with death or deadly suffering’?”

“No, venerable sir.”

“In the same way, Sunakkhatta, that bhikkhu practices restraint in the six fields of contact. Having understood that ‘acquisition is the root of suffering,’ being without acquisitions, liberated through the exhaustion of acquisitions, it is impossible that he would direct his body toward any acquisition or arouse his mind toward it.”

The Blessed One said this. Sunakkhatta, the son of the Licchavis, was delighted and pleased with the Blessed One’s words.
