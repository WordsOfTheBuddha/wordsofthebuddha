Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was wandering on a walking tour in the |Videha::name of an ancient kingdom in northeastern India, with its capital at Mithilā [videha]| country together with a large |Saṅgha::The community of monks and nuns practicing in line with the Buddha’s teachings. In the broader sense, this is the community of disciples who have realized the noble path and fruition through the Buddha’s teachings [saṅgha]| of five hundred bhikkhus.

Now at that time, the brahmin Brahmāyu was residing at |Mithilā::capital city of the Videha kingdom [mithilā]|. He was aged, old, elderly, advanced in years, and had reached the final stage of life—a hundred and twenty years old by birth. He was one who had mastered the |three Vedas::the primary sacred scriptures of the Vedic religion: Ṛgveda, Sāmaveda, and Yajurveda [tiṇṇaṁ vedānaṁ]|, together with their vocabularies, liturgy, phonology, and etymology, and with historical narratives as the fifth. He was a reciter of the Vedas, a grammarian and linguistic scholar, fully versed in |natural philosophy::science of disputation, cosmology; lit. extension of the world [lokāyata]| and the |auspicious marks of a great man::physical characteristics of an illustrious being [mahāpurisalakkhaṇa]|.

The brahmin Brahmāyu heard: “It seems the ascetic Gotama, the Sakyan son who went forth from the Sakyan clan, is wandering on a walking tour in the Videha country together with a large Saṅgha of five hundred bhikkhus. Now a good report about that sir Gotama has circulated thus: ‘The Blessed One is an |Arahant::a worthy one, a fully awakened being, epithet of the Buddha [arahant]|, a perfectly Awakened One, accomplished in true knowledge and conduct, who has reached the destination, knower of the world, an unsurpassed guide of trainable persons, a teacher of gods and humans, Buddha, Fortunate One. He declares this world with its |deities::gods, celestial beings [devas]|, |Māras::demons, tempters, beings of delusion; lit. causing death [mārake]|, |Brahmas::celestial beings residing in the Brahmā realms, often considered to be highly refined and long-lived deities [brahmā]|, this generation with its ascetics and brahmins, kings and commoners, which he has himself realized with |direct knowledge::experiential realization [abhiññā]|. He teaches the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]| that is beneficial in the beginning, beneficial in the middle, and beneficial in the end, meaningful and well-phrased, and he reveals a |spiritual life::a life of celibacy, contemplation, and ethical discipline lived for the sake of liberation; oriented toward inner development rather than sensual pleasures [brahmacariya]| that is entirely perfect and pure.’ It is auspicious to see such arahants.”

### Uttara’s Investigation

Now at that time, the brahmin Brahmāyu had a young brahmin student named Uttara, who had mastered the three Vedas, together with their vocabularies, liturgy, phonology, and etymology, and with historical narratives as the fifth; he was a reciter of the Vedas, a grammarian and linguistic scholar, fully versed in natural philosophy and the auspicious marks of a great man. Then the brahmin Brahmāyu addressed the young brahmin Uttara: “My dear Uttara, this ascetic Gotama, the Sakyan son who went forth from the Sakyan clan, is wandering on a walking tour in the Videha country together with a large Saṅgha of five hundred bhikkhus. Now a good report about that sir Gotama has circulated thus: ‘The Blessed One is an Arahant, a perfectly Awakened One ... It is auspicious to see such arahants.’ Come, my dear Uttara, approach the ascetic Gotama; having approached, find out about the ascetic Gotama—whether the report that has circulated about that sir Gotama is indeed so, or whether it is not so; whether that sir Gotama is such, or whether he is not such. Through you we shall know about that sir Gotama.”

“But how, sir, shall I find out about that sir Gotama—whether the report that has circulated about that sir Gotama is indeed so, or whether it is not so; whether that sir Gotama is such, or whether he is not such?”

“My dear Uttara, the thirty-two marks of a great man have been handed down in our hymns, and for a great man endowed with them, there are only two possible courses, no other. If he lives the household life, he becomes a |wheel-turning monarch::a just and virtuous universal ruler whose domain extends over the entire known world [rājā cakkavattī]|, a righteous king ruling by the Dhamma, a conqueror of the four quarters, who has established stability in the realm, endowed with the seven treasures. He has these seven treasures, namely: the wheel treasure, the elephant treasure, the horse treasure, the jewel treasure, the woman treasure, the householder treasure, and the counselor treasure as the seventh. Moreover, he has more than a thousand sons who are valiant, heroic in form, able to crush the armies of their enemies. Having conquered this earth bounded by the ocean without rod or sword, solely through the Dhamma, he rules over it. But if he goes forth from the household life into homelessness, he becomes an Arahant, a perfectly Awakened One, who draws back the veil from the world. Now I, my dear Uttara, am the giver of the hymns; you are the receiver of the hymns.”

“Yes, sir,” the young brahmin Uttara replied to the brahmin Brahmāyu. Rising from his seat, he paid homage to the brahmin Brahmāyu, and keeping him on his right, set out on a walking tour in the Videha country toward where the Blessed One was. Wandering by stages, he approached the Blessed One; having drawn near, he exchanged friendly greetings with the Blessed One. After having engaged in courteous and polite conversation, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the young brahmin Uttara scrutinized the thirty-two marks of a great man on the Blessed One’s body. The young brahmin Uttara saw the thirty-two marks of a great man on the Blessed One’s body for the most part, except for two. He had doubt and uncertainty, and was undecided and unsure about two marks of a great man: whether the private parts were enclosed in a sheath, and the largeness of the tongue.

Then it occurred to the Blessed One: “This young brahmin Uttara sees the thirty-two marks of a great man on me for the most part, except for two. He has doubt and uncertainty, and is undecided and unsure about two marks of a great man: whether the private parts are enclosed in a sheath, and the largeness of the tongue.”

Then the Blessed One performed such an act of psychic power that the young brahmin Uttara saw the Blessed One’s private parts enclosed in a sheath. Then the Blessed One extended his tongue, repeatedly touched and stroked both his ear-holes, repeatedly touched and stroked both his nostrils, and completely covered his entire forehead with his tongue.

Then it occurred to the young brahmin Uttara: “The ascetic Gotama is indeed endowed with the thirty-two marks of a great man. What if I were to follow the ascetic Gotama and observe his deportment?” Then the young brahmin Uttara followed the Blessed One for seven months like an ever-present shadow.

### The Thirty-Two Marks of a Great Man

Then, after the passage of seven months, the young brahmin Uttara set out on a walking tour in the Videha country toward Mithilā. Wandering by stages, he approached Mithilā and the brahmin Brahmāyu; having drawn near, he paid homage to the brahmin Brahmāyu and sat down to one side. While the young brahmin Uttara was seated to one side, the brahmin Brahmāyu said to him: “My dear Uttara, is the report that has circulated about that sir Gotama indeed so, and not otherwise? And is that sir Gotama truly such, and not otherwise?”

“The report that has circulated about that sir Gotama, sir, is indeed so, and not otherwise; and that sir Gotama is truly such, and not otherwise. Moreover, that sir Gotama is endowed with the thirty-two marks of a great man:

That sir Gotama, sir, has feet with level tread; this too is a mark of a great man of that great man, sir Gotama.

Beneath, on the soles of the feet of that sir Gotama, wheels have appeared with a thousand spokes, with rims and hubs, complete in every aspect ...

That sir Gotama has projecting heels ...

That sir Gotama has long fingers and toes ...

That sir Gotama has soft and tender hands and feet ...

That sir Gotama has net-like hands and feet ...

That sir Gotama has high-arched feet ...

That sir Gotama has calves like an antelope’s ...

While standing and without bending down, that sir Gotama touches and strokes his knees with the palms of both hands ...

That sir Gotama has private parts enclosed in a sheath ...

That sir Gotama is golden-complexioned, with skin like burnished gold ...

That sir Gotama has delicate skin; because of the delicacy of his skin, dust and dirt do not adhere to his body ...

That sir Gotama has body hairs growing one to each pore—single hairs growing in each hair pore ...

That sir Gotama has upward-pointing body hairs; the hairs that grow point upward, blue-black, the color of collyrium, curling in rings, turning to the right ...

That sir Gotama has a Brahmā-like upright frame ...

That sir Gotama has seven convexities [of the flesh] ...

That sir Gotama has a torso like a lion’s forepart ...

That sir Gotama has no furrow between his shoulders ...

That sir Gotama has the symmetrical proportions of a banyan tree: the span of his arms equals the height of his body, and the height of his body equals the span of his arms ...

That sir Gotama has evenly rounded shoulders ...

That sir Gotama has supreme |taste-sensitivity::unimpaired, pure channels of taste perception [rasaggasaggī]| ...

That sir Gotama has a jaw like a lion’s ...

That sir Gotama has forty teeth ...

That sir Gotama has even teeth ...

That sir Gotama has teeth without gaps ...

That sir Gotama has dazzling white eyeteeth ...

That sir Gotama has a large tongue ...

That sir Gotama has a divine voice like Brahmā’s, speaking with the melodious tone of a |cuckoo::the Indian cuckoo (karavīka), famed for its sweet call [karavika]| ...

That sir Gotama has deep blue eyes ...

That sir Gotama has eyelashes like a cow’s ...

Between the eyebrows of that sir Gotama grows a |tuft of hair::the uṇṇā mark on the forehead between the eyebrows [uṇṇā]|, white and resembling soft cotton down ...

That sir Gotama has a head like a |royal turban::a cranial protuberance or turban-shaped symmetry of the head [uṇhīsasīsa]|; this too is a mark of a great man of that great man, sir Gotama.

That sir Gotama, sir, is endowed with these thirty-two marks of a great man.

### The Buddha’s Deportment

When walking, that sir Gotama steps out first with his right foot. He does not lift his foot too far, nor does he set his foot down too close; he walks neither too quickly nor too slowly; he walks without knocking knee against knee, and without knocking ankle against ankle. When walking, he neither raises his thighs up, nor bends them down, nor brings them inward, nor splays them outward. When that sir Gotama walks, only the lower part of his body moves, and he does not walk with bodily strain. When looking around, that sir Gotama turns to look with his entire body; he does not look straight up, nor does he look straight down; he does not walk looking about this way and that, but looks a |yoke’s length::a distance of about two meters (four cubits) ahead [yugamatta]| before him; and beyond that, he has unobstructed |knowledge and vision::direct understanding and clear perception [ñāṇadassana]|.

When entering indoors, he neither raises his body up, nor bends it down, nor leans it inward, nor tilts it outward.

He turns around neither too far from the seat nor too close to it; he does not sit down on the seat leaning on his hand, nor does he fling his body onto the seat. While seated indoors, he does not engage in fidgeting with his hands or fidgeting with his feet; he does not sit with knee crossed over knee, nor with ankle crossed over ankle, nor does he sit supporting his chin with his hand. While seated indoors, he does not fear, does not tremble, does not waver, and is not agitated. He is fearless, untrembling, unwavering, unagitated, and without trepidation. And while seated indoors, that sir Gotama is devoted to |seclusion::inner solitude, mental detachment [viveka]|.

When receiving water for the alms bowl, he neither tips the bowl up, nor tips it down, nor tilts it inward, nor tilts it outward.

He receives neither too little nor too much water for the bowl. He does not wash the bowl making a sloshing sound, nor does he wash the bowl by twirling it around; he does not set the bowl down on the ground to wash his hands—when his hands are washed, the bowl is washed, and when the bowl is washed, his hands are washed. He pours out the water for the bowl neither too far nor too close, and without splashing it about. When receiving boiled rice, he neither tips the bowl up, nor tips it down, nor tilts it inward, nor tilts it outward. He receives neither too little nor too much boiled rice. Sir Gotama takes curry in suitable proportion, and does not exceed the mouthful of rice with curry. Sir Gotama turns the morsel over two or three times in his mouth before swallowing it, and no unchewed grain of rice enters his body, nor does any grain of rice remain in his mouth; only then does he bring another morsel to his lips. That sir Gotama partakes of food experiencing the taste, but not experiencing |passion::attachment, greed, infatuation [rāga]| for the flavor.

That sir Gotama partakes of food endowed with eight factors: ‘[This food is] not for amusement, not for intoxication, not for beautifying oneself, and not for adornment; but only for the support and maintenance of this body, for the ending of discomfort, and for assisting the spiritual life: thus I shall put an end to old feeling [of hunger] and shall not give rise to new feeling [from overeating], and I shall have sustenance of life, blamelessness, and a comfortable abiding.’

When he has finished his meal and receives water for the bowl, he neither tips the bowl up, nor tips it down, nor tilts it inward, nor tilts it outward. He receives neither too little nor too much water for the bowl. He does not wash the bowl making a sloshing sound, nor does he wash the bowl by twirling it around; he does not set the bowl down on the ground to wash his hands—when his hands are washed, the bowl is washed, and when the bowl is washed, his hands are washed. He pours out the water for the bowl neither too far nor too close, and without splashing it about.

When he has finished his meal, he sets the bowl down on the ground neither too far away nor too close; and he is neither careless with the bowl, nor overly |fussy::spending excessive time tending to it [ativelānurakkhī]| over the bowl.

When he has finished his meal, he sits in silence for a moment, but he does not let the time for the |blessing of appreciation::discourse of thanksgiving and merit-sharing offered after a meal [anumodana]| pass by. When he has finished his meal, he expresses appreciation: he neither criticizes that meal nor longs for another meal; rather, he instructs, encourages, inspires, and gladdens that assembly purely with a talk on the Dhamma. Having instructed, encouraged, inspired, and gladdened that assembly with a talk on the Dhamma, he rises from his seat and departs.

He walks neither too quickly nor too slowly, and he does not walk as one |anxious to get away::eager to be rid of the company [muccitukāma]|.

On the body of that sir Gotama, his robe is worn neither pulled up too high nor hanging down too low, neither clinging tightly to the body nor billowing away from the body; nor does the wind blow the robe off the body of that sir Gotama; nor do dust and dirt adhere to the body of that sir Gotama.

Having gone to the park, he sits down on the prepared seat. Having sat down, he washes his feet; yet that sir Gotama does not dwell devoted to the practice of beautifying his feet. Having washed his feet, he sits down folding his legs crosswise, setting his body upright, and establishing mindfulness |as his primary focal point::as first priority, directed towards the meditation object; lit. around the front [parimukhaṁ]|. He does not intend for his own affliction, nor for the affliction of others, nor for the affliction of both; that sir Gotama sits intending solely for his own welfare, the welfare of others, the welfare of both, and the welfare of the whole world. Having gone to the park, he teaches the Dhamma to the assembly: he neither flatters that assembly nor disparages that assembly; rather, he instructs, encourages, inspires, and gladdens that assembly purely with a talk on the Dhamma.

The sound that issues forth from the mouth of that sir Gotama is endowed with eight qualities: it is distinct, intelligible, melodious, pleasant to hear, |cohesive::compact, clear [bindu]|, |undispersed::not scattering outward [avisārī]|, deep, and resonant. That sir Gotama makes his voice understood in accordance with the size of the assembly, and his voice does not carry outside the assembly. Having been instructed, encouraged, inspired, and gladdened by that sir Gotama with a talk on the Dhamma, they rise from their seats and depart, looking back at him alone, reluctant to leave.

We have seen that sir Gotama walking, sir; we have seen him standing; we have seen him entering indoors; we have seen him seated indoors in silence; we have seen him eating indoors; we have seen him seated in silence after his meal; we have seen him expressing appreciation after his meal; we have seen him going to the park; we have seen him seated in silence at the park; we have seen him at the park teaching the Dhamma to the assembly. Such and such is that sir Gotama, and even more than that!”

When this was said, the brahmin Brahmāyu rose from his seat, arranged his upper robe over one shoulder, raised his joined palms toward the Blessed One, and uttered this inspired exclamation three times:

“Homage to the Blessed One, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One!

Homage to the Blessed One, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One!

Homage to the Blessed One, the Arahant, the perfectly Awakened One!

Perhaps some time or other we might meet with that sir Gotama! Perhaps there might be some conversation with him!”

### Brahmāyu Meets the Buddha

Then the Blessed One, wandering by stages on a walking tour in the Videha country, eventually arrived at Mithilā. There the Blessed One stayed at Mithilā in |Maghadeva’s Mango Grove::a royal mango park at Mithilā named after the ancient King Maghadeva [maghadevaambavana]|.

The brahmins and householders of Mithilā heard: “It seems the ascetic Gotama, the Sakyan son who went forth from the Sakyan clan, while wandering on a walking tour in the Videha country together with a large Saṅgha of five hundred bhikkhus, has arrived at Mithilā and is staying at Mithilā in Maghadeva’s Mango Grove. Now a good report about that sir Gotama has circulated thus: ‘The Blessed One is an Arahant, a perfectly Awakened One, accomplished in true knowledge and conduct, who has reached the destination, knower of the world, an unsurpassed guide of trainable persons, a teacher of gods and humans, Buddha, Fortunate One. He declares this world with its deities, Māras, Brahmas, this generation with its ascetics and brahmins, kings and commoners, which he has himself realized with direct knowledge. He teaches the Dhamma that is beneficial in the beginning, beneficial in the middle, and beneficial in the end, meaningful and well-phrased, and he reveals a spiritual life that is entirely perfect and pure.’ It is auspicious to see such arahants.”

Then the brahmins and householders of Mithilā approached the Blessed One. Having drawn near, some paid homage to the Blessed One and sat down to one side; some exchanged friendly greetings with the Blessed One, and after having engaged in courteous and polite conversation, sat down to one side; some raised their joined palms toward the Blessed One and sat down to one side; some announced their name and clan in the Blessed One’s presence and sat down to one side; some kept silent and sat down to one side.

The brahmin Brahmāyu heard: “It seems the ascetic Gotama, the Sakyan son who went forth from the Sakyan clan, has arrived at Mithilā and is staying at Mithilā in Maghadeva’s Mango Grove.” Then the brahmin Brahmāyu, together with several young brahmin disciples, approached Maghadeva’s Mango Grove.

Then, when the brahmin Brahmāyu was not far from the mango grove, it occurred to him: “It is not proper for me to approach to see the ascetic Gotama without having been announced beforehand.”

Then the brahmin Brahmāyu addressed a certain young brahmin: “Come, young brahmin, approach the ascetic Gotama; having approached, in my name ask whether the ascetic Gotama is free from illness and affliction, nimble, strong, and living comfortably, saying: ‘Sir Gotama, the brahmin Brahmāyu asks whether sir Gotama is free from illness and affliction, nimble, strong, and living comfortably.’ And say this: ‘Sir Gotama, the brahmin Brahmāyu is aged, old, elderly, advanced in years, and has reached the final stage of life—a hundred and twenty years old by birth. He is one who has mastered the three Vedas, together with their vocabularies, liturgy, phonology, and etymology, and with historical narratives as the fifth. He is a reciter of the Vedas, a grammarian and linguistic scholar, fully versed in natural philosophy and the auspicious marks of a great man. Among all the brahmins and householders residing in Mithilā, sir, the brahmin Brahmāyu is declared to be the foremost of them in wealth; the brahmin Brahmāyu is declared to be the foremost of them in the hymns; the brahmin Brahmāyu is declared to be the foremost of them in lifespan and fame. He wishes to see sir Gotama.’”

“Yes, sir,” that young brahmin replied to the brahmin Brahmāyu. He approached the Blessed One; having drawn near, he exchanged friendly greetings with the Blessed One. After having engaged in courteous and polite conversation, he stood to one side. Standing to one side, that young brahmin said to the Blessed One: “Sir Gotama, the brahmin Brahmāyu asks whether sir Gotama is free from illness and affliction, nimble, strong, and living comfortably, and he says this: ‘Sir Gotama, the brahmin Brahmāyu is aged, old, elderly, advanced in years, and has reached the final stage of life—a hundred and twenty years old by birth. He is one who has mastered the three Vedas, together with their vocabularies, liturgy, phonology, and etymology, and with historical narratives as the fifth. He is a reciter of the Vedas, a grammarian and linguistic scholar, fully versed in natural philosophy and the auspicious marks of a great man. Among all the brahmins and householders residing in Mithilā, sir, the brahmin Brahmāyu is declared to be the foremost of them in wealth; the brahmin Brahmāyu is declared to be the foremost of them in the hymns; the brahmin Brahmāyu is declared to be the foremost of them in lifespan and fame. He wishes to see sir Gotama.’” “Let the brahmin Brahmāyu come, young brahmin, whenever he thinks it is time.”

Then that young brahmin approached the brahmin Brahmāyu; having drawn near, he said to the brahmin Brahmāyu: “Permission has been granted by the worthy ascetic Gotama. You may proceed, sir, whenever you think it is time.”

Then the brahmin Brahmāyu approached the Blessed One. That assembly saw the brahmin Brahmāyu coming from afar. Seeing him, they stepped aside and made room at once, as one would for someone well-known and renowned.

Then the brahmin Brahmāyu said to that assembly: “Enough, sirs. Please sit on your own seats. I shall sit here in the presence of the ascetic Gotama.”

Then the brahmin Brahmāyu approached the Blessed One; having drawn near, he exchanged friendly greetings with the Blessed One. After having engaged in courteous and polite conversation, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the brahmin Brahmāyu scrutinized the thirty-two marks of a great man on the Blessed One’s body. The brahmin Brahmāyu saw the thirty-two marks of a great man on the Blessed One’s body for the most part, except for two. He had doubt and uncertainty, and was undecided and unsure about two marks of a great man: whether the private parts were enclosed in a sheath, and the largeness of the tongue. Then the brahmin Brahmāyu addressed the Blessed One in verses:

“Those two-and-thirty marks of which I’ve heard—
the marks belonging to a great man—
two of them I do not see
upon your body, Gotama.

Is that which should be concealed by cloth
enclosed within a sheath, O best of men?
Though called by a |word of the feminine gender::in Pāli, the noun ‘jivhā’ (tongue) is grammatically feminine; Brahmāyu plays on its gender to ask whether the Buddha’s tongue is short and unrevealing or expansive [nārīsamānasavhayā]|,
is your tongue perhaps not short?

Are you endowed with a large tongue,
as we have come to know of it?
Extend that ample tongue of yours—
dispel our doubt, O seer!

For welfare and benefit in this very life,
and for happiness in lives to come,
now that permission has been granted,
we ask whatever we desire to know.”

Then it occurred to the Blessed One: “This brahmin Brahmāyu sees the thirty-two marks of a great man on me for the most part, except for two. He has doubt and uncertainty, and is undecided and unsure about two marks of a great man: whether the private parts are enclosed in a sheath, and the largeness of the tongue.”

Then the Blessed One performed such an act of psychic power that the brahmin Brahmāyu saw the Blessed One’s private parts enclosed in a sheath. Then the Blessed One extended his tongue, repeatedly touched and stroked both his ear-holes, repeatedly touched and stroked both his nostrils, and completely covered his entire forehead with his tongue.

Then the Blessed One replied to the brahmin Brahmāyu in verses:

“Those two-and-thirty marks of which you’ve heard—
the marks belonging to a great man—
all of them are on my body:
do not have doubt anymore, brahmin.

What should be directly known has been known,
what should be cultivated has been cultivated,
what should be given up has been given up by me:
therefore, brahmin, I am an Awakened One.

For welfare and benefit in this very life,
and for happiness in lives to come,
now that permission has been granted,
ask whatever you desire to know.”

Then it occurred to the brahmin Brahmāyu:

“Permission has been granted to me by the ascetic Gotama. Now what should I ask the ascetic Gotama about: what is beneficial in this present life, or what pertains to lives to come?” Then it occurred to the brahmin Brahmāyu: “I am skilled in matters beneficial in this present life, and others too ask me about what is beneficial in this present life. Why don’t I ask the ascetic Gotama solely about the benefit that pertains to lives to come?”

Then the brahmin Brahmāyu addressed the Blessed One in verses:

“How, sir, does one become a brahmin?
And how does one become a |master of true knowledge::one who has penetrated spiritual knowledge; lit. knower of the Vedas [vedagū]|?
How, sir, does one have the |threefold knowledge::recollection of past lives, knowledge of the passing away and reappearance of beings, and knowledge of the exhaustion of the taints; contrasted with the Brahmanical three Vedas [tevijja]|?
And how is one called a |learned man::in Brahmanism, one versed in sacred learning (śrotriya); in the Buddha’s teaching, one purified of defilements [sotthiya]|?

How, sir, does one become an Arahant?
And how does one become |consummate::perfected, complete in the spiritual life [kevalī]|?
How, sir, does one become a sage?
And how is one declared a Buddha?”

Then the Blessed One replied to the brahmin Brahmāyu in verses:

“One who knows his former abodes,
and sees both heaven and states of loss,
who has reached the |end of birth::wearing away of rebirth [jātikkhaya]|—
a sage perfected through direct knowledge;

Who knows his mind is purified,
completely freed from every passion,
who has abandoned birth and death,
consummate in the spiritual life,
gone beyond all phenomena—
such a |steadfast one::unshakeable, equipoised amidst all worldly conditions [tādī]| is called a Buddha.”

When this was said, the brahmin Brahmāyu rose from his seat, arranged his upper robe over one shoulder, bowed down with his head at the Blessed One’s feet, covered the Blessed One’s feet with kisses, caressed them with his hands, and announced his name: “Sir Gotama, I am the brahmin Brahmāyu! Sir Gotama, I am the brahmin Brahmāyu!”

Then that assembly was struck with wonder and amazement, thinking: “How wonderful indeed, sirs! How marvelous indeed, sirs! That this brahmin Brahmāyu, so well-known and renowned, should display such supreme humility!” Then the Blessed One said to the brahmin Brahmāyu: “Enough, brahmin. Rise, sit down on your own seat, since your mind has confidence in me.” Then the brahmin Brahmāyu rose and sat down on his own seat.

### Brahmāyu’s Attainment and Final Destination

Then the Blessed One gradually gave the brahmin Brahmāyu a discourse, first on giving, then on virtue, and then on the heavens; he explained the danger, degradation, and defilement in sensual pleasures, and the benefit of renunciation. When the Blessed One knew that the mind of the brahmin Brahmāyu was ready, receptive, free of hindrances, uplifted, and confident, he then expounded the distinctive teaching of the Buddhas: suffering, its arising, its cessation, and the path. Just as a clean cloth with no dark spots would properly absorb dye, so too, in that very seat, the stainless, immaculate |Dhamma eye::stream-entry; experiential vision into the nature of reality [dhammacakkhu]| arose in the brahmin Brahmāyu: “Whatever is subject to arising is all subject to cessation.”

Then the brahmin Brahmāyu—having seen the Dhamma, attained the Dhamma, understood the Dhamma, deeply penetrated the Dhamma, having crossed beyond doubt, with no more uncertainty, confident and self-assured, and having become independent of others in the Teacher’s instruction—said to the Blessed One:

“Excellent, sir Gotama! Excellent, sir Gotama! Just as if, sir Gotama, one were to set upright what had been overturned, reveal what had been concealed, point out the way to one who was lost, or hold up a lamp in the dark so that those with eyes could see forms—even so, the Dhamma has been explained in many ways by sir Gotama. I go for refuge to sir Gotama, to the Dhamma, and to the Saṅgha of bhikkhus. May sir Gotama remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge from this day forth for life. And may sir Gotama, together with the Saṅgha of bhikkhus accept tomorrow’s meal from me.” The Blessed One consented in silence. Then the brahmin Brahmāyu, knowing that the Blessed One had consented, rose from his seat, paid homage to the Blessed One, and keeping him on his right, departed.

Then, after the passage of that night, the brahmin Brahmāyu had various kinds of excellent food prepared in his own residence and had the time announced to the Blessed One: “It is time, sir Gotama, the meal is ready.”

Then the Blessed One, having dressed early in the morning and taking his alms bowl and outer robe, approached the residence of the brahmin Brahmāyu; having arrived, he sat down on the prepared seat together with the Saṅgha of bhikkhus. Then, for seven days, the brahmin Brahmāyu personally served and satisfied the Saṅgha of bhikkhus headed by the Buddha with various kinds of excellent food.

Then, after the passage of those seven days, the Blessed One set out on a walking tour in the Videha country. And not long after the Blessed One had departed, the brahmin Brahmāyu passed away.

Then several bhikkhus approached the Blessed One; having drawn near, they paid homage to the Blessed One and sat down to one side. Seated to one side, those bhikkhus said to the Blessed One: “Venerable sir, the brahmin Brahmāyu has passed away. What is his destination? What is his future state?”

“The brahmin Brahmāyu|, bhikkhus,::::| was wise. He practiced |in accordance with the Dhamma::in line with the training guidelines of the Buddha’s teachings that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhammassānudhamma]| and did not pester me with the |technical points of the Dhamma::reasons or basis of the teaching [dhammādhikaraṇa]|. |Bhikkhus,::::| with the complete exhaustion of the |five lower fetters::personal existence view, doubt, clinging to rules and observances, sensual desire, and ill will [pañcorambhāgiyāni saṁyojanāni]|, the brahmin Brahmāyu is of |spontaneous birth::reborn in the Pure Abodes without parents, as a non-returner (anāgāmī) [opapātika]|, will attain final Nibbāna there, and is not liable to return from that world.”

The Blessed One said this. The bhikkhus were delighted and pleased with the Blessed One’s words.
