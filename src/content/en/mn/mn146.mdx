Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling at Sāvatthi, in Jeta’s Grove, Anāthapiṇḍika’s Park.

#### Nandaka Is Assigned to Advise the Nuns

Then Mahāpajāpatī Gotamī, together with about five hundred bhikkhunīs, approached the Blessed One. Having approached and paid homage to the Blessed One, she stood to one side. Standing to one side, |Mahāpajāpatī::Buddha's foster-mother; first Bhikkhunī [mahāpajāpati]| Gotamī said to the Blessed One: “Let the Blessed One advise the bhikkhunīs, venerable sir; let the Blessed One instruct the bhikkhunīs, venerable sir; let the Blessed One give a talk on the |Dhamma::teachings of the Buddha that point to the nature of reality, the ultimate truth [dhamma]| to the bhikkhunīs, venerable sir.”

Now on that occasion, elder bhikkhus were advising the bhikkhunīs in turn. But the venerable Nandaka did not wish to advise the bhikkhunīs in his turn.

Then the Blessed One addressed the venerable Ānanda: “Whose turn is it, Ānanda, to advise the bhikkhunīs today?”

“Everyone, venerable sir, has taken their turn to advise the bhikkhunīs. But this venerable Nandaka, venerable sir, does not wish to advise the bhikkhunīs in his turn.”

Then the Blessed One addressed the venerable Nandaka: “Advise the bhikkhunīs, Nandaka; instruct the bhikkhunīs, Nandaka; give a talk on the Dhamma to the bhikkhunīs, brahmin.”

### The First Day of Teaching

“Yes, venerable sir,” the venerable Nandaka replied to the Blessed One. Then, early in the morning, having dressed and taken his alms bowl and outer robe, he entered Sāvatthi for alms. Having wandered for alms in Sāvatthi and after the meal, having returned from his alms gathering, he went with a companion to where the Royal Park was. Those bhikkhunīs saw the venerable Nandaka coming from afar. Having seen him, they prepared a seat and set out water for the feet. The venerable Nandaka sat down on the prepared seat. Having sat down, he washed his feet. Those bhikkhunīs also, having paid homage to the venerable Nandaka, sat down to one side.

Seated to one side, the venerable Nandaka said to those bhikkhunīs: “Sisters, there will be a discussion by question and answer. There, if you understand, you should say: ‘We understand’; if you do not understand, you should say: ‘We do not understand.’ And if anyone has |doubt::uncertainty, perplexity, hesitation—especially in relation to faith, truth, or decision-making on the path [kaṅkhā]| or |uncertainty::indecision, confusion [vimati]|, I should be questioned right there: ‘How is this, venerable sir? What is the meaning of this?’”

“Even by this much, venerable sir, we are delighted and pleased with the venerable Nandaka, that the venerable Nandaka invites us in this way.”

#### Question and Answer on the Six Sense Bases

“What do you think, sisters, is the eye permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent—is it |unsatisfactory::uncomfortable, unpleasant [dukkha]| or |pleasant::comfortable, easy, good [sukha]|?”

“Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and |subject to change::of the nature of alteration, decay [vipariṇāmadhamma]|—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self’?”

“No, venerable sir.”

“What do you think, sisters, is the ear permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir ... is the nose permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “is the tongue permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “is the body permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “is the mind permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent—is it unsatisfactory or pleasant?”

“Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self’?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, we have already seen this as it actually is with right wisdom: ‘These |six internal sense bases::six inner sense spheres, the sense faculties (eye, ear, nose, tongue, body, mind) [cha + ajjhattika + āyatana]| are impermanent.’”

“Good, good, sisters! So it is for a disciple of the Noble Ones who sees as it actually is with right wisdom.

What do you think, sisters, are |forms::visible objects such as sights, material forms [rūpā]| permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent—is it unsatisfactory or pleasant?”

“Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self’?”

“No, venerable sir.”

“What do you think, sisters, are sounds permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir ... are smells permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “are tastes permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “are tangible objects permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “are mental objects permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent—is it unsatisfactory or pleasant?”

“Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self’?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, we have already seen this as it actually is with right wisdom: ‘These |six external sense bases::the six external sense objects—forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangibles, and mental objects—that correspond to the six internal sense bases and serve as their respective fields of experience [cha + bāhira + āyatana]| are impermanent.’”

“Good, good, sisters! So it is for a disciple of the Noble Ones who sees as it actually is with right wisdom.

What do you think, sisters, is |eye-consciousness::awareness of visible forms; it does not interpret or recognize meaning—only cognizes and distinguishes [cakkhuviññāṇa]| permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir.” “And that which is impermanent—is it unsatisfactory or pleasant?” “Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.” “And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self’?” “No, venerable sir.” “What do you think, sisters, is ear-consciousness permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir ... is nose-consciousness permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “is tongue-consciousness permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “is body-consciousness permanent or impermanent?” “Impermanent, venerable sir” ... “is mind-consciousness permanent or impermanent?”

“Impermanent, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent—is it unsatisfactory or pleasant?”

“Unsatisfactory, venerable sir.”

“And that which is impermanent, unsatisfactory, and subject to change—is it fitting to regard that as: ‘This is mine, this I am, this is my self’?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, we have already seen this as it actually is with right wisdom: ‘These six classes of consciousness are impermanent.’”

“Good, good, sisters! So it is for a disciple of the Noble Ones who sees as it actually is with right wisdom.

#### The Lamp, the Tree, and the Butcher’s Knife

Suppose, sisters, while an oil lamp is burning, its oil is impermanent and subject to change, its wick is impermanent and subject to change, its flame is impermanent and subject to change, and its radiance is impermanent and subject to change. If, sisters, someone were to say: ‘While this oil lamp is burning, its oil is impermanent and subject to change, its wick is impermanent and subject to change, and its flame is impermanent and subject to change; but whatever radiance it has, that is permanent, stable, eternal, and not subject to change’—would that person, sisters, be speaking rightly?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, while that oil lamp is burning, its oil is impermanent and subject to change, its wick is impermanent and subject to change, and its flame is impermanent and subject to change; all the more so is its radiance impermanent and subject to change.”

“In the same way, sisters, if someone were to say: ‘These six internal sense bases are impermanent; but whatever one experiences dependent on the six internal sense bases—whether pleasure, pain, or |neither-pleasure-nor-pain::neutral sensation, feeling which is neither unpleasant nor pleasant, neither comfortable nor uncomfortable sensation [adukkhamasukha]|—that is permanent, stable, eternal, and not subject to change’—would that person, sisters, be speaking rightly?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, dependent on the corresponding condition, the corresponding feelings arise. With the cessation of the corresponding condition, the corresponding feelings |cease::reach an end, stop [nirujjhati]|.”

“Good, good, sisters! So it is for a disciple of the Noble Ones who sees as it actually is with right wisdom.

Suppose, sisters, of a great tree standing sound and possessing heartwood, its roots are impermanent and subject to change, its trunk is impermanent and subject to change, its branches and foliage are impermanent and subject to change, and its shadow is impermanent and subject to change. If someone, sisters, were to say: ‘Of this great tree standing sound and possessing heartwood, its roots are impermanent and subject to change, its trunk is impermanent and subject to change, and its branches and foliage are impermanent and subject to change; but whatever shadow it has, that is permanent, stable, eternal, and not subject to change’—would that person, sisters, be speaking rightly?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, of that great tree standing sound and possessing heartwood, its roots are impermanent and subject to change, its trunk is impermanent and subject to change, and its branches and foliage are impermanent and subject to change; all the more so is its shadow impermanent and subject to change.”

“In the same way, sisters, if someone were to say: ‘These six external sense bases are impermanent; but whatever one experiences dependent on the six external sense bases—whether pleasure, pain, or neither-pleasure-nor-pain—that is permanent, stable, eternal, and not subject to change’—would that person, sisters, be speaking rightly?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, dependent on the corresponding condition, the corresponding feelings arise. With the cessation of the corresponding condition, the corresponding feelings cease.”

“Good, good, sisters! So it is for a disciple of the Noble Ones who sees as it actually is with right wisdom.

Suppose, sisters, a skilled butcher or their apprentice, having slaughtered a cow, were to carve it with a sharp butcher's knife, without damaging the inner mass of flesh and without damaging the outer hide. Whatever inner meat, inner sinews, and inner connective tissue there were, he would cut through, carve, slice, and sever all of that with the sharp butcher's knife. Having cut through, carved, sliced, and severed it, having removed the outer hide and covered that cow with that very same hide, he were to say: ‘This cow is joined to this very hide just as before’—would that person, sisters, be speaking rightly?”

“No, venerable sir.” “And why is that?” “Because, venerable sir, that skilled butcher or apprentice, having slaughtered a cow, carved it with a sharp butcher's knife without damaging the inner mass of flesh and without damaging the outer hide. Whatever inner meat, inner sinews, and inner connective tissue there were, he cut through, carved, sliced, and severed all of that with the sharp butcher's knife. Having cut through, carved, sliced, and severed it, having removed the outer hide and covered that cow with that very same hide—even though he might say: ‘This cow is joined to this very hide just as before,’ nevertheless that cow is disjoined from that hide.”

“This simile, sisters, has been made by me for the purpose of conveying a meaning. And this is the meaning here: ‘The inner mass of flesh,’ sisters, is a designation for the six internal sense bases. ‘The outer hide,’ sisters, is a designation for the six external sense bases. ‘The inner meat, inner |sinews::tendons and ligaments connecting muscle to bone [nhāru]|, and inner connective tissue,’ sisters, is a designation for |delight and lust::delight and desire; (comm) three karmic intentions [nandīrāga]|. ‘The sharp butcher's knife,’ sisters, is a designation for noble |wisdom::distinctive knowledge, discernment [paññā]|, by which noble wisdom one cuts through, carves, slices, and severs the inner defilements, inner fetters, and inner bonds.

There are these seven awakening factors, sisters, through the cultivation and frequent practice of which a bhikkhu, with the wearing away of the |taints::outflows, discharges; (comm) mental defilements [āsavā]|, realizes for himself with direct knowledge in this very life, the taintless |liberation of mind::mental liberation, emancipation of heart, a meditation attainment [cetovimutti]| and |liberation by wisdom::emancipation by insight [paññāvimutti]|, and having entered upon it, dwells in it. What seven? Here, sisters, a bhikkhu develops the |awakening factor of mindfulness::the quality of mindfulness leading to full awakening, remembering as a factor of enlightenment; first of the seven awakening factors [satisambojjhaṅga]|, which is |supported by seclusion::dependent on detachment, by means of disengagement [vivekanissita]|, |supported by dispassion::based on fading of desire [virāganissita]|, |supported by cessation::based on ending [nirodhanissita]|, and |culminates in complete relinquishment::ripens in release, culminating in letting go [vossaggapariṇāmī]|. He develops the |awakening factor of investigation of states::the quality of discriminative investigation into states, mental qualities and other phenomena; analyzing the teaching as a factor of enlightenment; second of the seven awakening factors [dhammavicayasambojjhaṅga]| ... the |awakening factor of energy::the quality of unrelenting effort, the sustaining power of persistence as a factor of enlightenment; third of the seven awakening factors [vīriyasambojjhaṅga]| ... the |awakening factor of joy::the quality of heartfelt gladness, spiritual rapture untainted by sensuality, as a factor of enlightenment; fourth of the seven awakening factors [pītisambojjhaṅga]| ... the |awakening factor of tranquility::the quality of inner stillness, bodily and mental calm, as a factor of enlightenment; fifth of the seven awakening factors [passaddhisambojjhaṅga]| ... the |awakening factor of collectedness::the quality of unification, stability, and deep stillness of the mind, as a factor of enlightenment; sixth of the seven awakening factors [samādhisambojjhaṅga]| ... the |awakening factor of equanimity::the quality of balanced observation, non-reactivity, and mental equipoise as a factor of enlightenment; seventh of the seven awakening factors [upekkhāsambojjhaṅga]|, which is supported by seclusion, supported by dispassion, supported by cessation, and culminates in complete relinquishment. These are the seven awakening factors, sisters, through the cultivation and frequent practice of which a bhikkhu, with the wearing away of the taints, realizes for himself with direct knowledge in this very life, the taintless liberation of mind and liberation by wisdom, and having entered upon it, dwells in it.”

Then the venerable Nandaka, having advised those bhikkhunīs with this advice, dismissed them: “Go, sisters, it is time.”

Then those bhikkhunīs, having delighted in and rejoiced in the venerable Nandaka’s words, rose from their seats, paid homage to the venerable Nandaka, and keeping him on their right, approached the Blessed One. Having approached and paid homage to the Blessed One, they stood to one side. Standing to one side, the Blessed One said to those bhikkhunīs: “Go, bhikkhunīs, it is time.”

Then those bhikkhunīs paid homage to the Blessed One, and keeping him on their right, departed.
