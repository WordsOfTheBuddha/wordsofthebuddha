Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling among the |Bhaggans::name of the people of Bhagga [bhaggā]| in |Crocodile Mountain::name of the capital city of Bhagga [susumāragira]|, in the Deer Park at Bhesakaḷā Grove.

Now at that time, a mansion named |Kokanada::Red Lotus [kokanada]| had recently been built for Prince Bodhi, which had not yet been occupied by any ascetic, brahmin, or human being.

Then Prince Bodhi addressed the young brahmin Sañjikāputta: “Come, dear Sañjikāputta, go to the Blessed One. When you have approached him, bow with your head at the Blessed One’s feet on my behalf, and ask whether he is well and healthy, nimble, strong, and dwelling comfortably, saying: ‘Venerable sir, Prince Bodhi bows with his head at the Blessed One’s feet, and asks whether he is well and healthy, nimble, strong, and dwelling comfortably.’ And say this: ‘Venerable sir, may the Blessed One consent to accept a meal from Prince Bodhi tomorrow together with the |Saṅgha::The community of monks and nuns practicing in line with the Buddha’s teachings. In the broader sense, this is the community of disciples who have realized the noble path and fruition through the Buddha’s teachings [saṅgha]| of bhikkhus.’”

“Yes, sir,” the young brahmin Sañjikāputta replied to Prince Bodhi. He approached the Blessed One, and having drawn near, exchanged friendly greetings with the Blessed One. After the exchange of a courteous and polite conversation, he sat down to one side. Seated to one side, the young brahmin Sañjikāputta said this to the Blessed One: “Prince Bodhi bows with his head at sir Gotama’s feet, and asks whether he is well and healthy, nimble, strong, and dwelling comfortably. And he says this: ‘May sir Gotama consent to accept a meal from Prince Bodhi tomorrow together with the Saṅgha of bhikkhus.’” The Blessed One consented in silence.

Then the young brahmin Sañjikāputta, having understood the Blessed One’s consent, rose from his seat and approached Prince Bodhi. Having drawn near, he said this to Prince Bodhi: “On your behalf, sir, we spoke to sir Gotama thus: ‘Prince Bodhi bows with his head at sir Gotama’s feet, and asks whether he is well and healthy, nimble, strong, and dwelling comfortably. And he says this—May sir Gotama consent to accept a meal from Prince Bodhi tomorrow together with the Saṅgha of bhikkhus.’ And the ascetic Gotama has consented.”

Then, after the night had passed, Prince Bodhi had various kinds of excellent food prepared in his own residence, had the Kokanada mansion spread with white cloths right down to the last flight of stairs, and addressed the young brahmin Sañjikāputta: “Come, dear Sañjikāputta, go to the Blessed One. Having approached him, announce the time to the Blessed One: ‘It is time, venerable sir, the meal is ready.’”

“Yes, sir,” the young brahmin Sañjikāputta replied to Prince Bodhi. He approached the Blessed One, and having drawn near, announced the time to the Blessed One: “It is time, sir Gotama, the meal is ready.”

Then the Blessed One, having dressed early in the morning, took his alms bowl and outer robe and went to Prince Bodhi’s residence.

Now at that time, Prince Bodhi was standing at the outer gateway, waiting for the Blessed One. Prince Bodhi saw the Blessed One coming from afar. Seeing him, he went out to meet the Blessed One, paid homage to him, and walking behind him, approached the Kokanada mansion. Then the Blessed One stood by the last flight of stairs.

### The White Cloths

Then Prince Bodhi said this to the Blessed One: “Venerable sir, may the Blessed One step onto the cloths, may the Accomplished One step onto the cloths, so that it may be for my welfare and happiness for a long time.” When this was said, the Blessed One remained silent.

For a second time ... For a third time, Prince Bodhi said this to the Blessed One: “Venerable sir, may the Blessed One step onto the cloths, may the Accomplished One step onto the cloths, so that it may be for my welfare and happiness for a long time.”

Then the Blessed One glanced at the venerable Ānanda. Then the venerable Ānanda said this to Prince Bodhi: “Have the cloths folded up, prince; the Blessed One will not step on a strip of cloth. The |Tathāgata::one who has arrived at the truth, an epithet of a perfectly Awakened One [tathāgata]| has |compassion::benevolence, concern, gentle regard [anukampā]| for future generations.”

Then Prince Bodhi had the cloths folded up and had seats prepared in the upper floor of the Kokanada mansion. Then the Blessed One ascended the Kokanada mansion and sat down on the prepared seat together with the Saṅgha of bhikkhus.

Then Prince Bodhi, with his own hands, served and satisfied the Saṅgha of bhikkhus headed by the Buddha with various kinds of excellent food. Then, when the Blessed One had finished his meal and withdrawn his hand from the bowl, Prince Bodhi took a low seat and sat down to one side.

### Pleasure Through Pain

Seated to one side, Prince Bodhi said this to the Blessed One: “Venerable sir, it occurs to me thus: ‘|Pleasure::comfort, contentedness, happiness, ease [sukha]| is not to be gained through pleasure; pleasure is to be gained through |pain::discomfort, unpleasantness, suffering [dukkha]|.’”

“Prince, before my |full awakening::perfect understanding, enlightenment [sambodha]|, while I was still an unawakened |Bodhisatta::Buddha before his awakening, aspirant Buddha [bodhisatta]|, it occurred to me too: ‘Pleasure is not to be gained through pleasure; pleasure is to be gained through pain.’

### The Two Teachers

Then, prince, at a later time, while I was still young, with black hair, endowed with the blessing of youth, and in the prime of life, although my mother and father wished otherwise and wept with tearful faces, I shaved off my hair and beard, put on the ochre robes, and went forth from the household life into |homelessness::lit. no home state [anagāriya]|. Thus, having gone forth, in search of what is |wholesome::healthy, beneficial, useful [kusala]|, seeking the unsurpassed state of sublime peace, I approached |Āḷāra Kālāma::name of the Buddha’s first teacher; lit. curved [āḷāra + kālāma]|. Having approached him, I said to Āḷāra Kālāma, ‘Friend Kālāma, I wish to lead the |spiritual life::a life of celibacy, contemplation, and ethical discipline lived for the sake of liberation; oriented toward inner development rather than sensual pleasures [brahmacariya]| in this teaching and training.’

When this was said, prince, Āḷāra Kālāma said to me, ‘The venerable one may stay here. This teaching is such that a wise person can soon enter upon and abide in it, realizing for themselves through |direct knowledge::experiential understanding [abhiññā]| their own teacher’s doctrine.’

Then, prince, before long, I quickly learned that teaching. And in so doing, prince, merely by repetition, recitation, and verbal expression, I claimed both knowledge and seniority, saying ‘I know, I see,’ along with others who claimed the same. Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘Āḷāra Kālāma does not proclaim that he abides in this Dhamma simply based on |faith::confidence, conviction, trust [saddhā]| alone, realizing it for himself with direct knowledge; surely, Āḷāra Kālāma abides knowing and seeing this Dhamma.’

So, prince, I went to Āḷāra Kālāma, and having approached him, I asked, ‘To what extent, friend Kālāma, do you proclaim that you have directly realized and abide in this teaching?’ When this was said, prince, Āḷāra Kālāma made known the |attainment of the base of nothingness::experience of the dimension of nothingness, a meditative absorption where one transcends all perception of material form, focusing instead on the experience of ‘nothingness’ [ākiñcaññāyatana]|.

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘It is not only Āḷāra Kālāma who has faith; I too have faith. It is not only Āḷāra Kālāma who has |energy::willpower, determination [vīriya]| ... |mindfulness::active presence of mind and lucid retention, recollection, keeping in mind [sati]| ... |collectedness::stability of mind, stillness of mind, mental composure [samādhi]| ... |wisdom::distinctive knowledge, discernment [paññā]|; I too have wisdom. Suppose I strive for the realization of the |Dhamma::teaching, state, doctrine [dhamma]| that Āḷāra Kālāma declares he enters upon and abides in by realizing for himself with direct knowledge.’ Then, prince, before long, very swiftly, I entered upon and abided in that Dhamma by realizing it for myself with direct knowledge.

Then, prince, I went to Āḷāra Kālāma, and having approached him, I asked, ‘Friend Kālāma, is it to this extent that you declare that you enter upon and abide in this Dhamma by realizing it for yourself with direct knowledge?’

‘Friend, this is indeed the extent to which I declare that I enter upon and abide in this Dhamma by realizing it for myself with direct knowledge.’

‘Friend, I too have entered upon and abided in this Dhamma by realizing it for myself with direct knowledge to this extent.’

‘Friend, it is our good fortune, truly our great fortune, that we see such a companion in the spiritual life as the venerable one. The Dhamma that I declare by realizing it for myself with direct knowledge, you abide in by realizing it for yourself with direct knowledge. The Dhamma that you abide in by realizing it for yourself with direct knowledge, I declare by realizing it for myself with direct knowledge. So the Dhamma I know, you also know; the Dhamma you know, I also know. Thus, as I am, so are you; as you are, so am I. Come now, friend, let us both lead this community together.’ Thus, prince, Āḷāra Kālāma, being my teacher, placed me, his student, as an equal to himself and honored me with great reverence.

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘This Dhamma does not lead to |disenchantment::de-illusionment, disinterest, dispassion [nibbidā]|, to the |fading of desire::dispassion, detachment [virāga]|, to |cessation::ending, termination [nirodha]|, to |tranquility::calmness, serenity, stillness, peace [upasama]|, to direct knowledge, to full awakening, to |Nibbāna::complete cooling, letting go of everything, deathless, freedom from calamity, the non-disintegrating; lit. blowing away [nibbāna]|, but only to reappearance in the base of nothingness.’ So, prince, without considering that Dhamma sufficient, I grew disenchanted with it and departed.

Then, prince, still in search of what is wholesome, seeking the unsurpassed state of sublime peace, I approached Uddaka |Rāmaputta::name of the Buddha’s second teacher; lit. Rāma’s son [rāmaputta]|. Having approached him, I said to Uddaka Rāmaputta, ‘Friend, I wish to lead the spiritual life in this teaching and training.’

When this was said, prince, Uddaka Rāmaputta said to me, ‘The venerable one may stay here. This teaching is such that a wise person can soon enter upon and abide in it, realizing for themselves through direct knowledge their own teacher’s doctrine.’

Then, prince, before long, I quickly learned that teaching. And in so doing, prince, merely by repetition, recitation, and verbal expression, I claimed both knowledge and seniority, saying ‘I know, I see,’ along with others who claimed the same.

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘Rāma did not proclaim that he abided in this Dhamma simply based on faith alone, realizing it for himself with direct knowledge; surely, Rāma abided knowing and seeing this Dhamma.’

So, prince, I went to Uddaka Rāmaputta, and having approached him, I asked, ‘Friend, to what extent did Rāma proclaim that he had directly realized and abided in this Dhamma?’ When this was said, prince, Uddaka Rāmaputta made known the |attainment of the base of neither perception nor non-perception::a meditative absorption where one’s awareness is so subtle that there is only a residue of formations, which is neither a clear perception or an absence of it [nevasaññānāsaññāyatana]|.

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘It was not only Rāma who had faith; I too have faith. It was not only Rāma who had energy ... mindfulness ... collectedness ... wisdom; I too have wisdom. Suppose I strive for the realization of the Dhamma that Rāma declares he enters upon and abides in by realizing for himself with direct knowledge.’ Then, prince, before long, very swiftly, I entered upon and abided in that Dhamma by realizing it for myself with direct knowledge.

Then, prince, I went to Uddaka Rāmaputta, and having approached him, I asked, ‘Friend, is this the extent to which Rāma declared that he entered upon and abided in this Dhamma by realizing for himself with direct knowledge?’

‘Friend, this is indeed the extent to which Rāma declared that he entered upon and abided in this Dhamma by realizing for himself with direct knowledge.’

‘Friend, I too have entered upon and abided in this Dhamma by realizing it for myself with direct knowledge to this extent.’

‘Friend, it is our good fortune, truly our great fortune, that we see such a companion in the spiritual life as the venerable one. The Dhamma that Rāma declared by realizing it for himself with direct knowledge, you abide in by realizing it for yourself with direct knowledge. The Dhamma that you abide in by realizing it for yourself with direct knowledge, Rāma declared by realizing it for himself with direct knowledge. So the Dhamma Rāma directly knew, you know; the Dhamma you know, Rāma directly knew. Thus, as Rāma was, so are you; as you are, so was Rāma. Come now, friend, you lead this community.’ Thus, prince, Uddaka Rāmaputta, though a fellow practitioner in the spiritual life, placed me in the position of teacher and honored me with great reverence.

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘This Dhamma does not lead to disenchantment, to the fading of desire, to cessation, to tranquility, to direct knowledge, to full awakening, to Nibbāna, but only to reappearance in the base of neither perception nor non-perception.’ So, prince, without considering that Dhamma sufficient, I grew disenchanted with it and departed.

Then, prince, still in search of what is wholesome, seeking the unsurpassed state of sublime peace, I gradually walked through |Magadha::An ancient kingdom in northeastern India (present day Bihar) and the setting for much of the Buddha’s teachings after his full awakening [magadha]| until I arrived at |Uruvelā::a region near the Nerañjarā river where the Buddha engaged in intensive meditation before attaining full awakening [uruvelā]|, near |Senānigama::a nearby village at Uruvelā, where the Buddha often went for alms. There I saw a delightful stretch of land, a graceful forest grove, with a river flowing nearby, clear and with beautiful, accessible banks, surrounded by a village suitable for alms.

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘This land is delightful, the forest grove is graceful, and a river flows nearby, clear and with beautiful, accessible banks, surrounded by a village suitable for alms. This is surely suitable for a young man of a good family intent on striving.’ So, prince, I sat down right there, thinking, ‘This is suitable for striving.’

### Three Similes

Now, prince, these three similes occurred to me spontaneously, never heard before.

Suppose, prince, there were a wet sappy piece of wood lying in water, and a man came with an |upper fire-stick::top piece of wood used in ancient fire-making rituals [uttarāraṇi]|, thinking: ‘I shall light a fire, I shall produce heat.’ What do you think, prince? Could the man light a fire and produce heat by taking the upper fire-stick and rubbing it against the wet sappy piece of wood lying in the water?”

“No, venerable sir. And why is that? Because, venerable sir, it is a wet sappy piece of wood, and furthermore it is lying in water. Eventually the man would reap only weariness and disappointment.”

“So too, prince, as to those ascetics and brahmins who still do not live bodily and mentally withdrawn from |sensual pleasures::sense desires, wishing, wanting, delighting in [kāma]|, and whose sensual desire, affection, infatuation, thirst, and |fever::mental torment, distress, strong desire, discomfort [pariḷāha]| for sensual pleasures has not been fully abandoned and dispelled internally—even if those good ascetics and brahmins feel painful, |agonizing::intense suffering, anguish [tibba]|, harsh, piercing feelings due to exertion, they are incapable of |knowledge::understanding, insight [ñāṇa]| and |vision::realization [dassana]|, of unsurpassed full awakening; and even if those good ascetics and brahmins do not feel painful, agonizing, harsh, piercing feelings due to exertion, they are incapable of knowledge and vision, of unsurpassed full awakening. This, prince, was the first simile that occurred to me spontaneously, never heard before.

Again, prince, a second simile occurred to me spontaneously, never heard before.

Suppose, prince, there were a wet sappy piece of wood lying on firm ground far from water, and a man came with an upper fire-stick, thinking: ‘I shall light a fire, I shall produce heat.’ What do you think, prince? Could the man light a fire and produce heat by taking the upper fire-stick and rubbing it against the wet sappy piece of wood lying on firm ground far from water?”

“No, venerable sir. And why is that? Because, venerable sir, it is a wet sappy piece of wood, even though it is lying on firm ground far from water. Eventually the man would reap only weariness and disappointment.”

“So too, prince, as to those ascetics and brahmins who live bodily and mentally withdrawn from sensual pleasures, but whose sensual desire, affection, infatuation, thirst, and fever for sensual pleasures has not been fully abandoned and dispelled internally—even if those good ascetics and brahmins feel painful, agonizing, harsh, piercing feelings due to exertion, they are incapable of knowledge and vision, of unsurpassed full awakening; and even if those good ascetics and brahmins do not feel painful, agonizing, harsh, piercing feelings due to exertion, they are incapable of knowledge and vision, of unsurpassed full awakening. This, prince, was the second simile that occurred to me spontaneously, never heard before.

Again, prince, a third simile occurred to me spontaneously, never heard before.

Suppose, prince, there were a dried-out, hollow piece of wood lying on firm ground far from water, and a man came with an upper fire-stick, thinking: ‘I shall light a fire, I shall produce heat.’ What do you think, prince? Could the man light a fire and produce heat by taking the upper fire-stick and rubbing it against the dried-out, hollow piece of wood lying on firm ground far from water?”

“Yes, venerable sir. And why is that? Because, venerable sir, it is a dried-out, hollow piece of wood, and furthermore it is lying on firm ground far from water.”

“So too, prince, as to those ascetics and brahmins who live bodily and mentally withdrawn from sensual pleasures, and whose sensual desire, affection, infatuation, thirst, and fever for sensual pleasures has been fully abandoned and dispelled internally—even if those good ascetics and brahmins feel painful, agonizing, harsh, piercing feelings due to exertion, they are capable of knowledge and vision, of unsurpassed full awakening; and even if those good ascetics and brahmins do not feel painful, agonizing, harsh, piercing feelings due to exertion, they are capable of knowledge and vision, of unsurpassed full awakening. This, prince, was the third simile that occurred to me spontaneously, never heard before. These, prince, were the three similes that occurred to me spontaneously, never heard before.

### Painful Striving

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘Suppose, with my teeth clenched and my tongue pressed against the roof of my mouth, I |restrain::control, suppress, hold back [abhiniggaṇhitabba]|, |subdue::squeeze, overcome [abhinippīḷetabba]|, and |overwhelm::crush, beat down [abhisantāpetabba]| mind with mind.’ So, prince, with my teeth clenched and my tongue pressed against the roof of my mouth, I restrained, subdued, and overwhelmed mind with mind. And as I clenched my teeth and pressed my tongue against the roof of my mouth, restraining, subduing, and overwhelming mind with mind, prince, sweat poured from my armpits. Just as, prince, a strong man might seize a weaker man by the head or shoulders and restrain, subdue, and overwhelm him, so too, prince, with my teeth clenched and my tongue pressed against the roof of my mouth, restraining, subduing, and overwhelming mind with mind, sweat poured from my armpits. And although unrelenting energy was aroused in me, prince, and |unmuddled::without confusion [asammuṭṭha]| mindfulness was established, yet my body was strained and not at ease, exhausted by that painful striving, overwhelmed by exertion.

Then, prince, it occurred to me: ‘Whatever ascetics or brahmins in the past experienced painful, agonizing, harsh, piercing feelings due to exertion, this is the utmost, there is nothing beyond it. ### A Pleasure Apart from Sensual Pleasures

Standing to one side, my nurse said this to the Blessed One on my behalf: ‘Venerable sir, this Prince Bodhi goes for refuge to the Blessed One, to the Dhamma, and to the Saṅgha of bhikkhus. And now, dear Sañjikāputta, for a third time I go for refuge to the Blessed One, to the Dhamma, and to the Saṅgha of bhikkhus. May the Blessed One remember me as a lay follower who has gone for refuge from this day forth for life.”
