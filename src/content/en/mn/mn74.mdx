---
title: Dīghanakha sutta - To Dīghanakha
description: The Buddha shows the wanderer Dīghanakha how obstinately clinging to the view that “nothing is acceptable” inevitably causes disputes and vexation. He then teaches the impermanence of the body and the naturally wearing-away nature of feelings. Hearing this, Sāriputta attains full liberation through non-clinging, while Dīghanakha attains the stainless Dhamma eye.
qualities: wrong view, attachment, stubbornness, giving up, free from attachment, felt experience, recognition of impermanence, recognition of unsatisfactoriness, recognition of not-self, disenchantment, liberation, direct knowledge, vision, arahant, stream-entry
theme: cultivating discernment, wisdom

Thus have I heard—At one time, the Blessed One was dwelling at |Rājagaha::name of a city; capital of Magadha; lit. king’s house [rājagaha]| in the Boar’s Cave on the |Vulture’s peak::name of a mountain peak around Rājagaha; lit. vulture’s peak [gijjhakūṭa]| mountain.

Then the wanderer |Dīghanakha::name of a wandering ascetic; lit. long nails [dīghanakha]| approached the Blessed One. Having drawn near, he exchanged greetings with the Blessed One. After the exchange of courteous and polite conversation, he stood to one side. Standing to one side, the wanderer Dīghanakha said to the Blessed One, “Sir Gotama, I assert this, I hold this view: ‘Nothing is acceptable to me.’”

“This view of yours, |Aggivessana::name of a Brahman family clan; lit. descendant of Aggivessa [aggivessana]|: ‘Nothing is acceptable to me’—is this view also not acceptable to you?”

“If this view of mine were acceptable to me, sir Gotama, it too would be the same, it too would be the same.”

“Well, Aggivessana, there are plenty in the world who say: ‘It too would be the same, it too would be the same,’ yet they do not abandon that view and they take up another view. There are few in the world, Aggivessana, who say: ‘It too would be the same, it too would be the same,’ and who abandon that view and do not take up another view.

“There are, Aggivessana, some ascetics and brahmins who assert this and hold this view: ‘Everything is acceptable to me.’ There are, Aggivessana, some ascetics and brahmins who assert this and hold this view: ‘Nothing is acceptable to me.’ There are, Aggivessana, some ascetics and brahmins who assert this and hold this view: ‘Some things are acceptable to me, some things are not acceptable to me.’ Among them, Aggivessana, the view of the ascetics and brahmins who hold: ‘Everything is acceptable to me’ is close to |lust::infatuation, intense desire, obsession, passion [sārāga]|, close to bondage, close to delight, close to holding on, close to clinging. Among them, Aggivessana, the view of the ascetics and brahmins who hold: ‘Nothing is acceptable to me’ is close to non-lust, close to |not being bound::non-attachment; lit. not yoking together [asaññoga]|, close to non-delight, close to not holding on, close to |non-clinging::non-attachment, being without grasping, lit. not taking near [anupādāna]|.”

When this was said, the wanderer Dīghanakha said to the Blessed One: “Sir Gotama praises my viewpoint! Sir Gotama fully praises my viewpoint!”

“Among them, Aggivessana, regarding the ascetics and brahmins who assert and hold this view: ‘Some things are acceptable to me, some things are not acceptable to me’—whatever is acceptable to them, that view is close to lust, close to bondage, close to delight, close to holding on, close to clinging. Whatever is not acceptable to them, that view is close to non-lust, close to not being bound, close to non-delight, close to not holding on, close to non-clinging.

Now, Aggivessana, a wise person among those ascetics and brahmins who assert and hold the view ‘Everything is acceptable to me’ considers thus: ‘If I obstinately adhere to and insist upon the view “Everything is acceptable to me” and declare: “Only this is true, anything else is wrong,” then I may clash with the two others: with an ascetic or brahmin who asserts and holds the view “Nothing is acceptable to me,” and with an ascetic or brahmin who asserts and holds the view “Some things are acceptable to me, some things are not acceptable to me.” I may clash with these two, and when there is a clash, there are disputes; when there are disputes, there are quarrels; when there are quarrels, there is vexation.’ Thus, foreseeing for himself clashes, disputes, quarrels, and vexation, he abandons that view and does not take up another view. This is how there comes to be the abandoning of these views; this is how there comes to be the |relinquishing::letting go, releasing [paṭinissagga]| of these views.

A wise person among those ascetics and brahmins who assert and hold the view ‘Nothing is acceptable to me’ considers thus: ‘If I obstinately adhere to and insist upon the view “Nothing is acceptable to me” and declare: “Only this is true, anything else is wrong,” then I may clash with the two others: with an ascetic or brahmin who asserts and holds the view “Everything is acceptable to me,” and with an ascetic or brahmin who asserts and holds the view “Some things are acceptable to me, some things are not acceptable to me.” I may clash with these two, and when there is a clash, there are disputes; when there are disputes, there are quarrels; when there are quarrels, there is vexation.’ Thus, foreseeing for himself clashes, disputes, quarrels, and vexation, he abandons that view and does not take up another view. This is how there comes to be the abandoning of these views; this is how there comes to be the relinquishing of these views.

A wise person among those ascetics and brahmins who assert and hold the view ‘Some things are acceptable to me, some things are not acceptable to me’ considers thus: ‘If I obstinately adhere to and insist upon the view “Some things are acceptable to me, some things are not acceptable to me” and declare: “Only this is true, anything else is wrong,” then I may clash with the two others: with an ascetic or brahmin who asserts and holds the view “Everything is acceptable to me,” and with an ascetic or brahmin who asserts and holds the view “Nothing is acceptable to me.” I may clash with these two, and when there is a clash, there are disputes; when there are disputes, there are quarrels; when there are quarrels, there is vexation.’ Thus, foreseeing for himself clashes, disputes, quarrels, and vexation, he abandons that view and does not take up another view. This is how there comes to be the abandoning of these views; this is how there comes to be the relinquishing of these views.

Now, Aggivessana, this body, made of material form, consisting of the four great elements, procreated by a mother and father, and built up out of rice and porridge, subject to impermanence, to being worn and rubbed away, to injury and destruction; should be regarded as |impermanent::not lasting, transient, unreliable [anicca]|, |unsatisfactory::uncomfortable, unpleasant [dukkha]|, a disease, a boil, a dart, misery, an affliction, alien, disintegrating, empty, and |not-self::not suitable to identify with, impersonal, non-subjective, without an essence [anatta]|. When one regards this body as impermanent, as unsatisfactory, as a disease, a boil, a dart, misery, an affliction, alien, disintegrating, empty, and as not-self, whatever bodily desire, bodily affection, and bodily subservience one has is abandoned.

There are, Aggivessana, these three feelings: pleasant feeling, painful feeling, and neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. Aggivessana, at the time one feels a pleasant feeling, one does not feel a painful feeling or a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling; one only feels a pleasant feeling at that time. Aggivessana, at the time one feels a painful feeling, one does not feel a pleasant feeling or a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling; one only feels a painful feeling at that time. Aggivessana, at the time one feels a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, one does not feel a pleasant feeling or a painful feeling; one only feels a neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling at that time.

Pleasant feeling, Aggivessana, is impermanent, |constructed::compounded, conditioned, fabricated [saṅkhata]|, |dependently arisen::casually produced, arisen together from a cause [paṭiccasamuppanna]|, naturally wearing away, subject to disintegration, subject to fading away, having the nature to end. Painful feeling, Aggivessana, is impermanent, constructed, dependently arisen, naturally wearing away, subject to disintegration, subject to fading away, having the nature to end. Neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling, Aggivessana, is impermanent, constructed, dependently arisen, naturally wearing away, subject to disintegration, subject to fading away, having the nature to end.

Seeing thus, Aggivessana, a learned disciple of the Noble Ones becomes |disenchanted with::disillusioned with [nibbindati]| pleasant feeling, disenchanted with painful feeling, and disenchanted with neither-painful-nor-pleasant feeling. Being disenchanted, he |becomes dispassionate::is detached [virajjati]|. Through dispassion, the mind is liberated. When liberated, there is the knowledge that [the mind] is liberated.

He knows clearly: ‘Birth is exhausted, the spiritual life has been lived, what had to be done has been done, there is no more coming to any state of existence.’

A bhikkhu whose mind is thus liberated, Aggivessana, does not side with anyone, does not |dispute::argue, disagree [vivadati]| with anyone; he uses the speech of the world without clinging to it.”

At that time, the venerable Sāriputta was standing behind the Blessed One, fanning the Blessed One. Then this thought occurred to the venerable Sāriputta: “The Blessed One speaks of the abandoning of these various things through |direct knowledge::experiential realization [abhiññā]|; the Fortunate One speaks of the relinquishing of these various things through direct knowledge.” As the venerable Sāriputta reflected on this, his mind was liberated from the taints through non-clinging.

Moreover to the wanderer Dīghanakha, there arose the |stainless::dustless, pure, untainted [viraja]|, immaculate Dhamma eye: ‘Whatever is subject to arising, is subject to cessation.’ Then the wanderer Dīghanakha—having seen the |Dhamma::the ultimate truth that the Buddha’s teachings point to [dhamma]|, attained the Dhamma, understood the Dhamma, deeply penetrated the Dhamma, having crossed beyond doubt, with no more uncertainty, confident and self-assured, and become independent of others in the Teacher’s instruction—said to the Blessed One:

Excellent, sir Gotama! Excellent, sir Gotama! Just as if one might set upright what had been overturned, |reveal::uncover [vivarati]| what had been concealed, point out the way to one who was lost, or hold up a lamp in the dark so that those with eyes could see forms, in the same way, sir Gotama, the Dhamma has been |explained::illustrated [pakāsita]| by you in many ways. I go for refuge to venerable Gotama, to the Dhamma, and to the |Saṅgha::The community of monks and nuns practicing in line with the Buddha’s teachings. In the broader sense, this is the community of disciples who have realized the noble path and fruition through the Buddha’s teachings [saṅgha]| of bhikkhus. May sir Gotama remember me as a lay follower who, from this day forward, has gone to refuge for life.”
